The dispute over whether Poland trains too many doctors has a precedent. At the turn of the 1980s and 1990s admissions to medicine were cut from over 6,000 places to about 2,000 and kept at that level for more than a decade. The pre-cut level returned after 31–36 years, depending on which places are counted. France and Portugal did the same, and the effects of those decisions are visible in the medical workforce to this day.

We describe how much is known about the Polish cut and where the numbers come from, what trace it left in the register of doctors and what similar decisions looked like in two other countries. At the end we return to the analysis published by the Supreme Medical Chamber (NIL) in 2024, which proposed cutting a second time, and to the question of what basis is used to set this number at all.

## How Poland cut admissions

According to Rynek Zdrowia, the number of first-year places in medicine was reduced gradually from over 6,000 in 1987 to about 3,300 in 1990, and as late as 2003 there were 2,090 places in day programmes in medicine.[^rz2019] Polityka Zdrowotna writes that in 1994–2000 just over 2,000 people a year were admitted.[^pz] The largest part of the cut therefore fell within three years: between 1987 and 1990 admissions shrank by about 45%. Then for more than a decade they stayed near the bottom. From 1987 to 2003 the fall was at least 65% — “at least”, because the starting point is “over 6,000” places.

<Aside title="Where the numbers from the 1980s and 1990s come from">

- **Only from the press.** The numbers for 1987, 1990 and 2003 were given in 2019 by Rynek Zdrowia, and those for 1994–2000 by Polityka Zdrowotna. Neither publication named a primary source. Nor are they confirmed by any legal act or Statistics Poland (GUS) yearbook we have found.
- **2,090 is a number not verified in the act.** The Minister of Health’s regulation on limits for 2003 exists, but its annexes were published separately and are not in the text of the act in the Sejm database.[^dzu2003]
- **Different definitions.** The numbers for 1987 and 1990 concern first-year places, those for 2003 — day programmes, and from 2005 — full-time programmes in Polish. The chart below therefore shows the shape of the changes, not a series comparable to the last place.

</Aside>

Why was the cut made? In the same Rynek Zdrowia article a former president of the Supreme Medical Council (in 2010–2018) explained that politicians considered the number of graduates too large. This “overproduction” was linked to Poland being treated as the “medical back-up for the operations of the Warsaw Pact” (our translation), while the country was emerging from poverty and assessed health needs differently.[^rz2019] This is an explanation given almost three decades later, not a document from the time of the decision.

### The recovery took more than three decades

The Ministry of Health writes that it has been increasing admission limits for medicine since 2005.[^mz] In full-time programmes the recovery was slow: 2,360 places in 2005, 3,023 in 2010[^fa] and 3,589 in 2015/16.[^mnisw]

How many years the return took depends on which places are compared with 1987. Full-time programmes in Polish, together with the pool for foreigners, exceeded 6,000 places only in 2023/24 — there were 6,707 of them then, 36 years after 1987.[^mnisw] If fee-paying part-time programmes in Polish are added, the threshold was crossed in 2018/19, after 31 years: the limit after the autumn amendment gave 6,123 places in Polish.[^lim2018] Which measure corresponds better to the 1987 number is not known, because we do not know its definition.

The total limit, including part-time programmes and programmes in a foreign language, is {total('2026/27')} places in 2026/27, of which {polish('2026/27')} are in Polish.[^lim2026] How it grew year by year is shown in a table in the analysis [English Division](/en/analysis/english-division-medical-studies/), and why there is still a shortage of doctors — in the analysis [Why Poland is short of doctors](/en/analysis/why-poland-is-short-of-doctors/). Who sets the limit and what role the medical self-government plays in it is described in the text on [restricted access to the profession](/en/mechanisms/restricted-access-to-the-profession/).

## A gap in the register of doctors

The effects of the cut can be looked for in the age structure of doctors. The Central Register of Doctors gives the number of practising doctors in each five-year age group. Among doctors aged 26–70 the smallest group is those aged 46–50, the cohorts that started their studies roughly in the second half of the 1990s, when admissions were at their lowest.[^crl]

There are 11,607 doctors aged 46–50. That is {gapOlder}% fewer than among those aged 56–60 (15,963), who started their studies before the cut, and {gapYounger}% fewer than among those aged 26–30 (20,346), who entered medical school after the limits were rebuilt.[^crl] The gap falls in the middle of the professional career.

The smaller number of doctors is not due to smaller birth cohorts. The 41–45 age group, also one of the least numerous (12,787 doctors), consists of people born roughly in 1981–1985, at the peak of the last baby boom. In 1983 nearly 724,000 children were born in Poland.[^gus] The constraint was therefore not the number of young people but the number of university places.

The cut in limits is not, however, the only explanation for the gap. The same former president of the Supreme Medical Council pointed out that a clear staffing collapse came after Poland joined the European Union, when doctors began leaving more often.[^rz2019] In 2004 today’s 46–50-year-olds were 24–28, finishing their studies or internship, and the 41–45-year-olds were 19–23 and at university. Part of the gap is therefore emigration, not only admissions. Public data do not allow these two causes to be separated, because they do not show how many doctors from these cohorts left for good.

## Portugal: admissions down by three-quarters

In Portugal in the early 1970s it was judged that there were too many students in medical schools, which made, among other things, hospital-based teaching difficult. The government introduced a numerus clausus, and according to a study by EDULOG admissions were restricted too much and for too long. In 1979 and 1980 the global limit for medicine was 805 places, and in 1986 it fell to 190 — by 76%. In 1999/00 it stood at 70% of the 1979 level, and it exceeded that level for the first time in 2000/01, after 21 years. Since 2010/11 it has remained at about 1,700 places a year.[^edulog]

The trace of this decision is visible in the workforce. A 2013 study by the University of Coimbra for the Portuguese medical association called it a “generational gap”: doctors aged 40–49 made up only 15% of all doctors.[^coimbra] According to data from the Portuguese patient register of 24 August 2026, about 1.36 million people were waiting to be assigned a family doctor.[^dn] The number cannot be directly compared with earlier, higher estimates, because in 2026 the way patients are classified in the register was changed — part of the fall is due to that change.

Portugal also teaches caution with the “doctors per 1,000 population” indicator. In OECD statistics it has 5.8, but this is the number of doctors licensed to practise, including retired people and those who do not practise. The OECD itself notes that this number overstates the system’s capacity.[^chp] When the Portuguese statistical office counted practising doctors, it found 46,131 in 2024.[^eco] The same pitfall applies to Poland — see [Why Poland is short of doctors](/en/analysis/why-poland-is-short-of-doctors/).

## France: a 61% cut and a correction ten years late

France introduced a numerus clausus in medicine by an act of 1971, with limits from 1972. According to the OECD the official aim was to match the number of students to the training capacity of hospitals, and a less openly stated one — to limit the number of doctors and the pressure on spending in a system that pays for each service.[^oecd] Admissions fell from nearly 9,000 people a year in the mid-1970s to 3,500, or by 61%.[^oecd] According to the French National Academy of Medicine, the bottom, 3,500 places, came in 1992. The limit should have been rising from the late 1980s, but it was raised only ten years later — to 7,500 places in 2007.[^academie] By 2011 it had returned to 8,000.[^oecd]

The effects are visible to this day. According to the French health statistics office (DREES), there were 318 doctors per 100,000 population in 2021, compared with 325 in 2012. Standardised density, which takes population ageing into account, is projected to fall until 2028 and to return to the 2021 level only around 2035. A scenario with admissions 20% higher speeds up the way out of the trough by about three years — it does not remove it.[^drees]

In 2019 France abolished the numerus clausus. Five years later the French Court of Audit (Cour des comptes) found that there is still no common method for estimating health needs, so it cannot be guaranteed that the current targets are right.[^cdc]

In none of the three cases was the stated reason a calculation of the population’s health needs. What decided was the capacity of universities and hospitals, spending or a political judgement, and the effects appeared after a dozen or more years, because that is how long it takes from admission to medical school to the title of specialist (see [Why Poland is short of doctors](/en/analysis/why-poland-is-short-of-doctors/)). In each of these countries the limit was set by the state, not by the medical self-government.

## The 2024 proposal for a second cut

In June 2024 the Supreme Medical Chamber published an analysis prepared by the then chair of its team for the transformation of the healthcare system. The chamber stated that it was not the position of the Supreme Medical Council. According to the team’s model, in 2048 Poland will need about 5.14 doctors per 1,000 population (in a range of 4.8–5.4), and with the limits proposed by the ministry at the time it would reach 6.3, about 20% above need. The cost of training the surplus doctors was estimated at up to <Money value={15000000000} scale="billion" /> over the forecast period.[^nil]

The analysis presented three variants and did not say which one it recommended:[^nil]

- **Variant A:** keep the 7,797 places in programmes in Polish from the draft regulation for 2024/25, and from 2027 “cut the number of places abruptly by almost half” (our translation), to about 4,100 a year.
- **Variant B:** reduce the number of places every year by about 9%, to about 4,200 a year from 2030.
- **Variant C:** reduce the limit by 1,500 places already in 2024, to about 6,300, and then by 4% a year in 2026–2034, to about 4,200.

Poland passed the level of about 4,100 places during the first cut roughly in 1988–1989, between over 6,000 in 1987 and about 3,300 in 1990.[^rz2019] This is an approximate comparison: the analysis counts all places in Polish, while the numbers from the 1980s concern the first year of studies.

The text of the analysis says nothing at all about doctors’ pay — we checked its version on the chamber’s website on 16 October 2026. Its arguments are the cost of training and the balance between the supply of doctors and need. The analysis itself also refers to the experience of other countries: it warns against a “yo-yo effect”, that is fluctuations in limits set without prior analysis, and calls for a plan of the number of places 5–10 years ahead.[^nil]

None of the variants came into force. The final version of the regulation for 2024/25 gave {polish('2024/25')} places in Polish instead of the 7,797 in the draft; in 2025/26 there were {polish('2025/26')}, and in 2026/27 — {polish('2026/27')}.[^limity] In June 2026, however, the Ministry of Health announced that the limit no longer needed to be increased and that it might fall in the coming years.[^rz2026] The dispute over the direction continues, and the medical self-government gives its opinion on successive drafts — we describe this in the text on [restricted access to the profession](/en/mechanisms/restricted-access-to-the-profession/).

## The “show the model” test

The chamber and the ministry reach a similar conclusion. In November 2025 the Ministry of Health forecast that by 2034 the supply of doctors would exceed need, assuming that a doctor works on average 161 hours a month. It wrote: “The challenge for healthcare in the future will therefore not be staff shortages among doctors” (our translation).[^mz] It added the caveat that shortages may occur in some regions and fields. We write more about this forecast in the analysis [Why Poland is short of doctors](/en/analysis/why-poland-is-short-of-doctors/).

The history of the three countries yields a simple test for any proposal to change the limit, up or down: show the model. What health needs do you assume, on what data, with what working time per doctor, with what breakdown by region and field — and what happens when these assumptions are changed.

- **The chamber’s 2024 analysis** describes results and variants, but the chamber’s website has neither the input data nor a calculation that would allow it to be reproduced. Its author wrote, at the same time, that the discussion should be about the assumptions adopted and the mechanics of the model.[^nil]
- **The ministry** does not give the forecasting method in its November 2025 announcement.[^mz] On the BASiW portal, however, it publishes an interactive report, “<span lang="pl">Model popytowo-podażowy lekarzy</span>” (“Supply and demand model for doctors”), with definitions, data sources and scenarios.[^basiw] Whether it allows the result to be recalculated on one’s own assumptions, we have not established.

After fifty years of regulating admissions, France still has no common method for estimating needs.[^cdc] Without a public model it is impossible to check whether a proposed number follows from patients’ needs or from something else — and a mistake comes to light after a dozen or more years. How a change in admissions translates into the number of doctors in the following decades can be checked in the [doctor supply simulator](/analizy/symulator-podazy-lekarzy/) (in Polish).
