Sweden has no sickness funds and no health contribution. Treatment is organised and paid for from their own tax by 21 regions, and most doctors are their employees. We describe how this system works according to Swedish acts and official data, and check what is public in it. The pay of a doctor employed by a region is a document available to anyone, every month the state publishes how the statutory treatment deadlines are being met, and every year — how many doctors have lost their licence to practise. Transparency ends where a doctor works for a private company paid by the region.

## Regions instead of funds

The law obliges a region to provide good healthcare to everyone who lives in it.[^hsl] The regions finance and run most treatment, 290 municipalities are responsible for the care of older and disabled people, and the state sets the legal framework and supervises.[^profil]

The rest is added by the state: in 2024 the regions received <Money value={57000000000} currency="SEK" scale="billion" decimals={0} /> in general and equalisation grants and <Money value={28000000000} currency="SEK" scale="billion" decimals={0} /> in targeted grants.[^skr-okt] The regions’ budgets are tight. In June 2025 a government commission that examined whether the state should take over responsibility for healthcare proposed instead a larger role for the state in selected areas, including workforce training, medicines and screening.[^komisja]

## What it costs

Sweden spends more on health than the European Union average, and the share of public money is the highest among the countries described.[^eurostat-sha]

<CountryBars title="Current health expenditure in 2024 (Eurostat)" codes={['SE', 'PL', 'EU']} keys={['healthGdp', 'perCapitaEur', 'perCapitaPps', 'publicShare', 'oopShare']} />

According to the profile by the OECD and the European Commission, in 2023 public money covered 99% of the cost of hospital treatment and 93% of outpatient treatment, but only 54% of the cost of medicines and 42% of dental care.[^profil]

## A public payer, private health centres

A region is at once the payer and the owner of the hospitals. In primary care, however, the law requires it to give the patient a choice of health centre and to admit private providers on the same terms as its own.[^hsl] According to the statistics office, private providers accounted for 18% of healthcare activity in 2024 and employed 21.0% of its staff.[^scb-prywatne] In primary care the share is larger: according to the Ekonomifakta service, in 2025 48% of health centres were private (564 of 1,176) — 71% in the Stockholm region, 13% in Västerbotten.[^prywatne]

The terms on which a region admits providers must be announced on a national website.[^lov] How much a particular company receives follows from the region’s documents, which are public on request.[^tf] We found no open register of the regions’ payments to individual companies.

## What the patient pays

Fees are set by each region: in 2026 a visit to a health centre costs from <Money value={200} currency="SEK" /> to <Money value={400} currency="SEK" />, and a visit to a specialist from <Money value={200} currency="SEK" /> to <Money value={460} currency="SEK" />.[^avgifter] In Stockholm a visit to a doctor at a health centre and to a specialist costs <Money value={275} currency="SEK" /> in 2026, and a day in hospital <Money value={130} currency="SEK" />; children and young people under 18 do not pay.[^oplaty]

A patient’s annual spending is limited by two national caps: <Money value={1450} currency="SEK" /> for visits and <Money value={3800} currency="SEK" /> for prescription medicines.[^limity]

According to data cited by the public broadcaster, 826,442 people had a private policy that shortens the route to a specialist at the end of 2025; it is often paid for by the employer.[^polisy]

Doctors in hospitals and public health centres are employees of the region. There is no pay table — pay is set individually — but the statistics office reports averages every year.[^scb]

The difference between women and men is small among specialists: <Money value={97300} currency="SEK" /> and <Money value={98700} currency="SEK" />.[^scb] According to the OECD, a salaried specialist in Sweden earns 2.17 times as much as the average worker (2024) — less than in Poland, where, however, this measure covers only employment contracts.[^oecd-remun]

The Swedish counterpart of Polish civil-law (B2B) contracts is doctors hired from staffing agencies. In 2023 the regions spent <Money value={9277000000} currency="SEK" scale="million" decimals={0} /> on agency staff, or 5.1% of the cost of their own staff. Since January 2024 the regions have bought such services under a joint agreement.[^slf] In 2024 spending fell to <Money value={5953000000} currency="SEK" scale="million" decimals={0} />, of which <Money value={3829000000} currency="SEK" scale="million" decimals={0} /> went on doctors, and in 2025 by a further 20%. The association of regions sets these costs against a level of 2% of staff costs.[^agencje] The doctors’ union says this has not been followed by more salaried doctors: in its survey about four in ten respondents judged that total staffing had decreased.[^slf]

## What is public

**The pay of every employee of a region.** The principle of public access to official documents is laid down in Sweden in an act of constitutional rank. Anyone can request a document, and the authority has no right to establish who the applicant is or what they need the information for.[^tf] Information on the pay of an employee of an authority or a region is such a document.[^st] This is transparency on request, not a public register — and it covers only people employed by a public body.

**Income in the tax return.** The tax agency discloses on request the assessed income from work of every person. From income for 2023 onwards the scope was narrowed: only the total amount is now public, without a breakdown by source.[^skatt]

**Treatment deadlines.** The law guarantees contact with a health centre on the same day, a medical assessment within 3 days, and a visit to a specialist and a procedure within 90 days. The agency Socialstyrelsen publishes every month how often the deadlines are met.[^terminy]

**Revoked licences to practise.** Every year a state board publishes the number of doctors and nurses whose licence to practise it has revoked or restricted, broken down by reason.[^hsan]

**The cost of agency doctors.** Every year the association of regions publishes total spending on agency staff, separately for doctors and nurses.[^agencje]

Outside the principle of public access are doctors employed in private health centres and companies, even if these are paid for entirely by the region.[^st] The additional work of the regions’ doctors is also hard to see: an audit in the Västerbotten region showed in 2023 that nearly 400 of about 1,500 doctors had such work, and in a sample of 60 people 47 did not have the required documentation.[^bisyssla]

## How many doctors there are and how long people wait

In May 2025 there were 46,020 doctors working in healthcare, 13% more than five years earlier. The distribution is uneven: from 342 to 555 doctors per 100,000 inhabitants depending on the region.[^nps] About 1,700 people start medical studies each year,[^uka] and about 30% of doctors were trained abroad. All regions report shortages of family doctors and specialists.[^profil] In 2025, 66% of inhabitants had a regular doctor assigned by name.[^staly]

Sweden measures waiting times and does not hide the result. In September 2026, 74% of first visits to a specialist and 65% of operations and procedures took place within the statutory deadline of 90 days. When a region misses the deadline, it must provide treatment with another provider at no extra cost to the patient.[^terminy] [^hsl] According to the government, the number of people waiting more than 90 days fell by 41% between October 2022 and July 2026.[^kolejki]

In 2025, 2.1% of Swedes went without a medical visit because of cost, distance or waiting time — almost exclusively because of waiting time (1.7%). In Poland the figure was 2.9%, including 2.2% because of waiting time.[^eurostat-silc]

Deaths from treatable causes are deaths before the age of 75 that could have been avoided with good and timely treatment. Sweden has the fewest of the countries described; the European Union average is 86.8 per 100,000 inhabitants.[^wyniki] We write about the opinion surveys [separately](/en/analysis/satisfaction-with-healthcare-in-poland/).

## Professional and criminal liability

**Professional.** Sweden has no medical courts. Supervision is exercised by the state inspectorate IVO, and the licence to practise is decided on by the state board HSAN. Since 2011 disciplinary penalties such as a warning are no longer imposed: the board can set a three-year probation period, restrict the right to prescribe medicines or revoke the licence to practise.[^psl] A provider is obliged to report to the inspectorate every event that caused or could have caused serious harm to a patient — in order to establish the cause in the organisation of work, not the person at fault.[^psl]

In 2025 the board revoked the licence to practise of 64 doctors, including 4 temporarily (a year earlier 48, including 2 temporarily), restricted the right to prescribe medicines of 16, and set a probation period for 38. It upheld the inspectorate’s applications in 97% of cases.[^hsan] These numbers cannot be compared directly with the Polish ones: the grounds for revocation include not only gross errors but also criminal offences, addictions, illness and failure to complete the probation plan, and some doctors give up their licence to practise at their own request ([a broader comparison with Poland](/en/analysis/doctor-discipline-licence-revocation/)).[^hsan]

**Criminal.** For an error, medical staff are liable under the general provisions of the criminal code: on causing death by negligence and on causing bodily injury or illness by negligence, which carries a fine or up to six months’ imprisonment, and in serious cases up to four years. Causing death by negligence carries up to three years’ imprisonment, and in serious cases — after the provision was tightened in 2026 — from two to six years.[^bb] The inspectorate is obliged to notify the prosecution service when it suspects an offence punishable by imprisonment.[^psl] In 2025 it did so 109 times, a year earlier 136 — according to the inspectorate, these mainly concerned repeated suspicions of irregularities in the handling and prescribing of medicines.[^ivo]

We found no statistics on indictments and convictions for treatment errors. According to the Swedish medical journal, specialised prosecutors handled 154 such cases in 2013, of which a handful went to court.[^akty]

**Compensation.** The patient does not have to prove anyone’s fault. The patient injury act gives a right to compensation if the injury could have been avoided, and it is paid by an insurer owned by the regions. In 2025 there were 22,800 claims, compensation was awarded in more than 40% of the cases decided, and payments amounted to nearly <Money value={740000000} currency="SEK" scale="million" decimals={0} />.[^lof] We write more about this model in the text on [compensation without establishing fault](/en/mechanisms/no-fault-compensation-and-doctors-liability/).

Swedish transparency works because a doctor has one employer and one salary. In Poland most specialists work on [civil-law (B2B) contracts](/en/mechanisms/contract-vs-employment/), and one person can have them in several facilities, so even full transparency of a single contract would not show the whole. Sweden also shows what happens to the cost of “hired” labour when payers start buying it jointly and publishing the spending.
