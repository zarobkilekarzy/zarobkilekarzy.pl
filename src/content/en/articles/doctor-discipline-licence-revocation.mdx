## Who judges doctors

The medical self-government consists of the Supreme Medical Chamber (NIL), 23 regional medical chambers and the Military Medical Chamber, which has the status of a regional chamber.[^nil-2024] Its members are doctors and dentists entered on the chambers’ membership lists. The licence to practise is granted by a chamber, and on receiving it a doctor is entered on the list of its members.[^uil-ustroj]

The Act on medical chambers gives the self-government two roles at once: it represents the people practising the profession and, at the same time, oversees its proper practice within the limits of the public interest.[^uil-ustroj] The second role is served by two bodies that every chamber has: a disciplinary prosecutor (*rzecznik odpowiedzialności zawodowej*) and a medical court. The path of a case step by step, with examples from rulings, is described in our text [How medical courts work](/en/mechanisms/how-medical-courts-work/).

Disciplinary prosecutors, their deputies and the members of the medical courts are elected for a four-year term by medical congresses: regional congresses in the regional chambers and the National Congress in the Supreme Medical Chamber. Any doctor who has practised for at least 10 years may stand.[^uil-sady] It is therefore doctors elected by doctors who bring the charges and decide the cases. When adjudicating, members of the medical courts are bound by generally applicable law.[^uil-sady]

A case passes through the successive stages set out in the act:[^uil-postepowanie]

<Steps>

1. **Complaint.** A patient files it with the regional disciplinary prosecutor of the chamber to which the doctor belongs; the prosecutor may also act on receiving information about possible misconduct. Proceedings are not opened if 3 years have passed since the act, and once opened, liability lapses after 5 years (for a criminal offence — not earlier than its limitation period).
2. **Preliminary investigation.** It is conducted by the prosecutor — first verification steps, then, if there are grounds, a preliminary investigation. It should last no more than 6 months but may be extended. The injured party is a party to the proceedings and may challenge a refusal to open proceedings before the medical court.
3. **First instance.** If the prosecutor considers the charges justified, they submit a request for a penalty to the regional medical court and act as prosecutor before it. The court sits as a panel of three.
4. **Appeal.** Within 14 days a party may appeal to the Supreme Medical Court. It sits as a panel of five and may uphold, amend or set aside the decision.
5. **Cassation.** Against a final decision of the Supreme Medical Court that ends the case, the parties, the minister of health and the president of the Supreme Medical Council may lodge a cassation appeal with the Supreme Court within 2 months. This is the only stage at which the case is examined by a court outside the self-government.

</Steps>

Where the evidence points to serious misconduct and the doctor’s continued work endangers patients, the medical court may, at the prosecutor’s request, temporarily suspend the licence to practise or restrict the scope of practice — for up to a year, with immediate effect and with the possibility of extension after the court examines the case again.[^uil-postepowanie] Cases of doctors who cannot practise because of their state of health are handled by the self-government under a separate procedure, outside the medical courts.[^nil-2024][^uil-postepowanie]

The Patient Ombudsman, too, refers patients in such matters to the regional disciplinary prosecutor.[^rpp] How to prepare a complaint and what other routes exist is described in our guide [Patients’ rights](/en/patient-rights/).

## Seven penalties

Article 83 of the act lists seven penalties, from the mildest:[^uil-kary]

1. admonition,
2. reprimand,
3. fine — from one third to four times the average monthly wage in the enterprise sector,
4. ban on holding managerial positions in healthcare, for 1–5 years,
5. restriction of the scope of professional practice, for 6 months to 2 years,
6. suspension of the licence to practise, for 1–5 years,
7. revocation of the licence to practise.

The court may add a ban on holding managerial positions to a restriction of the scope of practice or to a suspension, and a fine to penalties four to six. With penalties four to seven it may also order the decision to be published in the chamber’s bulletin.[^uil-kary]

The most important line runs between the sixth and the seventh penalty. A suspension lasts at most five years and is expunged five years after it has been served. Revocation of the licence to practise is the only one of the seven penalties that is never expunged.[^uil-kary]

## How many times the licence to practise was revoked

A decision of a regional medical court does not close the case, because it can be appealed.[^uil-postepowanie] That is why we count final revocations: those that nobody appealed, and those that the Supreme Medical Court upheld or ordered itself.

In 2000–{LAST_YEAR} the regional medical courts ordered revocation of the licence to practise {FIRST_INSTANCE_TOTAL} times: {FIRST_INSTANCE[0].n} times in {FIRST_INSTANCE[0].period}, {FIRST_INSTANCE[1].n} times in {FIRST_INSTANCE[1].period} and {FIRST_INSTANCE[2].n} times in {FIRST_INSTANCE[2].period}.[^nil-sady] Final revocations can be counted exactly from {FIRST_YEAR}: in {allYears} there were {plTotal}, against {firstSince2010} first-instance decisions over the same period.[^suma-nil] The older report form, used in 2000–2009, does not separate final decisions from the others. At first instance the medical courts ordered revocation {FIRST_INSTANCE_2000_2009} times in those years, so over the whole quarter-century there were at least {plTotal} final revocations, but the reports do not give the exact number.[^nil-sady] In 2019 Prawo.pl, a Polish legal news site, reported that 13 doctors had lost their licence to practise since the 1990s.[^prawo2019]

The reports do not break down the difference between {firstSince2010} and {plTotal}. It consists of decisions that the Supreme Medical Court amended or set aside, and of cases in which an appeal is still pending, especially from recent years. The years do not match either: we count a revocation in the year it became final, not in the year of the first decision.

The table in the next section shows this year by year. In {PL_ZERO_YEARS} of the {YEARS_COUNT} years there were no final revocations at all, and in {PL_ONE_YEARS} there was one each. In 2020–2022 there were {total('pl', 2020, 2022)}, in 2023 {plIn(2023)}, and in 2024 {plIn(2024)} — more than half as many as in 2015–2023 combined ({total('pl', 2015, 2023)}).[^nil-sady] For {LAST_YEAR} the reports show none, even though the regional courts ordered {lastFirstInstance} revocations that year. None of them became final that year without an appeal. If the Supreme Medical Court upholds them at second instance, they will appear in the statistics for later years.[^nil-2025]

A picture of a single year at second instance comes from the Supreme Medical Court’s report for 2022. Decisions ending the proceedings, issued at a hearing, concerned 97 doctors that year, and orders issued at sittings — 185. The harshest penalties imposed by that court in 2022 were suspensions (6 doctors) and restrictions of the scope of practice (5). Only some cases reach the courts. ## The criminal court is a separate route

Proceedings before a medical court run independently of criminal proceedings.[^uil-postepowanie] A criminal court may ban a doctor who has committed a crime from practising the profession as a penal measure — if they abused their profession in committing the crime or if their continued practice endangers important legally protected interests.[^kk]

The low number of revocations within the self-government therefore does not tell the whole story about doctors convicted of crimes, because in their cases a ban on practising may be imposed by a criminal court. We did not find how many doctors have received such a ban in the sources we use. The figures in this text concern the medical courts only.

## Poland compared with Norway, Sweden, the United Kingdom and Czechia

In Norway a doctor’s authorisation, that is the right to practise, is withdrawn by the state supervisory authority Helsetilsynet.[^helse] In Sweden applications are made by, among others, the state inspectorate IVO, and the decision is taken by the state board HSAN.[^hsan] Both countries publish the number of such decisions every year, separately for each profession.

In the United Kingdom complaints about doctors are investigated by the General Medical Council, the statutory regulator that keeps the register of doctors. Whether to erase a doctor from the register is decided by the Medical Practitioners Tribunal Service, which according to the GMC is operationally separate from it and decides independently. The GMC publishes the number of erasures every year in its fitness to practise statistics, and information about tribunal hearings and their outcomes is published.[^gmc]

Poland has about {formatEN(PL_DOCTORS / NO_DOCTORS[LAST_YEAR], 1)} times as many doctors as Norway and about {formatEN(PL_DOCTORS / SE_DOCTORS_2025, 1)} times as many as Sweden, and the United Kingdom about {formatEN(UK_DOCTORS_2024 / PL_DOCTORS, 1)} times as many as Poland,[^lekarze][^gmc-kadry] which is why we compare figures per 100,000 doctors.

These figures are not directly comparable. In Poland, revocation of the licence to practise is a penalty for professional misconduct. Crimes are tried separately by a criminal court, and the self-government deals with unfitness to work on health grounds under a different procedure. The authorities in Norway and Sweden withdraw licences for various reasons under a single procedure, and in Sweden a licence may also be withdrawn at the request of the doctor themselves.[^hsan] The Norwegian authority reports voluntary surrenders of authorisation separately.[^helse-2025] The British data are tribunal decisions in the year they were taken, which can be appealed to a court, not final decisions; they do not include erasures ordered on review of earlier sanctions or erasures at the doctor’s own request.[^gmc] The denominators come from different registers. That is why we compare orders of magnitude, not results exact to a single case.

The closest equivalent of the Polish revocation is erasure from the British register. In the United Kingdom it is also a sanction for misconduct: cases concerning only a doctor’s health, as the GMC writes, never end in erasure.[^gmc] The tribunal erased doctors less often than the Scandinavian authorities, about {formatEN(RATES.uk, 0)} erasures a year per 100,000 doctors, but still about {ratioUk} times as often as Polish medical courts. The years 2020–2021 were unusual there: during the pandemic the tribunal held fewer hearings.[^gmc]

The Czech Medical Chamber (ČLK), like the Polish self-government, is a professional body. It does not publish annual statistics, but according to its president it expelled 10 doctors over 20 years, and expulsion is possible for at most five years.[^tempus] For Germany and Austria there is no comparable figure. In 2026 the German federal government told the Bundestag that it has no national statistics.[^de] The Austrian act on the medical profession prohibits informing the public about the course and outcome of closed disciplinary proceedings.[^at]

## The self-government’s arguments

There are arguments that explain the low number of revocations and that need to be set out. Penalties are graduated, and revocation of the licence to practise is meant to be a last resort: the court should first consider a milder penalty. This is how an expert in medical law described the principle in a statement for MedExpress.[^medexpress] Revocation is also the only penalty that is never expunged, so it remains in the register of penalised doctors for good.[^uil-kary]

The reports do not say what the cases were about, so the figures alone cannot show whether the penalties matched the seriousness of the misconduct. The structure of penalties fits both the explanation that most cases concern less serious misconduct and the claim that the courts rule leniently.

The self-government’s decisions are also subject to external review: a cassation appeal to the Supreme Court may be lodged not only by a party but also by the minister of health.[^uil-postepowanie] NIL also runs a portal of final rulings of the medical courts, whose rules were adopted in March 2024. It publishes anonymised rulings there and, on its website, tables with the number of final rulings since 2023, broken down since 2024 into penalties, discontinuations and acquittals.[^nil-2024][^portal] It has not yet prepared the table for the second half of 2025 — according to NIL, because the courts exceed the deadlines for drafting written reasons.[^portal]

## What can be checked

Penalties imposed on individual doctors go into the Register of Penalised Doctors, kept by the Supreme Medical Council. Information from the register is given only to a person or institution that shows a legal interest.[^rejestr]

Aggregate data are public but scattered. NIL publishes the medical courts’ reports as separate spreadsheet and document files, year by year.[^nil-sady] To count how many doctors lost their licence to practise with final effect, one has to add up two sections of the reports — those of the regional medical courts and of the Supreme Medical Court — for all 24 chambers. In two files an incorrect total cell also has to be ignored.[^suma-nil] Norway and Sweden publish this figure ready-made, every year and broken down by profession, and the British regulator — every year in its table of tribunal outcomes.[^helse][^hsan][^gmc]

We propose the same principle — public data without names — for public money: [the total paid to each person from public money, under a permanent identifier, without names](/en/demand/).

## Method

We calculated the Polish figures ourselves from NIL files: the annual reports of the regional medical courts and of the Supreme Medical Court for {allYears} and the summary of rulings from 2000–2014.[^nil-sady] We add up all 24 chambers, always from the columns of the individual chambers rather than from the total cell in the file, because in two reports that cell is wrong.[^suma-nil] A final revocation is a decision of a regional court that nobody appealed (section 8.9 of the report), or a revocation upheld or ordered by the Supreme Medical Court at second instance (section 8.11). We assume that these two sets do not overlap: a case with an appeal ends with a decision of the Supreme Medical Court, so it should not appear in section 8.9 of the regional courts’ report. This cannot be checked case by case, because the reports do not give case numbers. Revocations in both sections in the same year occurred only in 2016 and 2024. If in both of those years the same doctor was involved, there would be {plIn(2024) - 1} instead of {plIn(2024)} for 2024, and {plTotal - 2} instead of {plTotal} for {allYears}.[^pokrycie] We calculate the structure of penalties from first-instance decisions in {PENALTIES.period}. In the regional courts’ report for {LAST_YEAR} the column of the Koszalin chamber is empty, although in 2019–2024 that court had a dozen or so cases a year, so the totals for {LAST_YEAR} are incomplete.[^nil-2025]

The rate per 100,000 doctors is the annual average for {span} divided by the number of doctors. For Norway the denominator is the average number of employed doctors over those years. For Sweden it is an estimate of that average, because the Socialstyrelsen report gives the figure for May {LAST_YEAR} and the change since 2020, not an annual series. The Swedish figure covers only doctors employed in healthcare, the Norwegian one all employed doctors.[^lekarze] For the United Kingdom we take doctors with a licence to practise and estimate the average for the window as the mean of the 2020 figure ({formatEN(UK_DOCTORS_2020)}) and the 2024 figure ({formatEN(UK_DOCTORS_2024)}), because the GMC has not yet published the number for {LAST_YEAR}.[^gmc-kadry] For Poland we take {formatEN(PL_DOCTORS)} practising doctors according to the NIL register at the end of 2025, because we do not have an annual series from that register.[^lekarze] If there had been on average one fifth fewer doctors in {span}, the Polish rate would be about {formatEN(rate(plWindow, PL_DOCTORS * 0.8), 1)}, so the order of magnitude does not change.

The Polish denominator does not include dentists, while the medical courts’ reports do include them and do not show them separately in the rows on penalties. In 2020–2025 dental specialties accounted for 14 to 20% of the requests for a penalty for which the reports give the specialty.[^nil-sady] If dentists were added to the denominator, the Polish rate would be even lower. Swedish and Norwegian statistics list dentists separately, so they are not included in the comparison there.[^hsan][^helse-2025] In the Swedish series for 2013 we use the figure from the HSAN report (25), although Läkartidningen gives 17.[^hsan]
