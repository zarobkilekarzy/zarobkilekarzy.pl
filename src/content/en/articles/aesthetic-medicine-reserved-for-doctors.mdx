Since 2023 the rules have provided for a separate medical skill: “aesthetic and restorative medicine” (*medycyna estetyczno-naprawcza*). In January 2026 the Ministry of Health explained that procedures with botulinum toxin, fillers or lasers are healthcare services that a cosmetologist may not perform. Cosmetologists who performed them for years want this reversed.

In this dispute an argument also comes up that concerns patients of the National Health Fund (NFZ). If the market for aesthetic procedures is reserved for doctors, some of their hours may go there instead of to public care. This is a hypothesis. Nobody measures or publishes how many doctors’ hours actually shift outside the public system.

## What the rules say

A regulation of the minister of health of 13 June 2023 added “aesthetic and restorative medicine”, with code 028, to the list of professional skills of doctors and dentists.[^rozp] The skill is confirmed by a certificate. The scope of procedures is described by the minimum certification standard, which the Centre of Postgraduate Medical Education (CMKP) published in July 2025.[^br-raport]

In January 2026 the Ministry of Health issued a statement in which it classed these procedures as healthcare services and indicated who may perform them.[^rp][^doz] The statement is not a new provision. In it the ministry relied on the Act on the professions of doctor and dentist and the 2023 regulation,[^rp] and the trade magazine Wiadomości Kosmetyczne judged that it confirms the existing legal position.[^wk]

Not every doctor has this entitlement. According to the statement, it belongs to specialists in two fields and to doctors who have obtained the certificate.[^rp][^prawo]

The standard covers, among other things:[^prawo][^doz]

- procedures with botulinum toxin;
- fillers: cross-linked hyaluronic acid, calcium hydroxylapatite, poly-L-lactic acid and polycaprolactone;
- needle mesotherapy, for example with non-cross-linked hyaluronic acid, vitamins or peptides;
- procedures with platelet-rich plasma and fibrin;
- procedures with medical devices: lasers, IPL, radiofrequency and HIFU;
- medium-depth and deep medical peels;
- threads, sclerotherapy, lipotransfer, injection lipolysis and the treatment of complications after procedures.

The standard does not cover microneedle mesotherapy or procedures with devices that the manufacturer has approved for use by people who are not doctors.[^prawo][^doz] Who may work with a given device is therefore decided by its documentation.[^wk]

## The dispute with cosmetologists

Cosmetology is taught at university, but the profession of cosmetologist is not regulated by any act. The Beauty Razem Foundation (Fundacja Beauty Razem), which represents the beauty industry, appealed to the prime minister in November 2025 to delete code 028 from the regulation. It argues that complications are caused by people without relevant education, not by cosmetologists with a degree.[^br-apel]

Patient safety speaks in favour of the restriction. Many of these procedures are injections or procedures with high-energy lasers,[^doz] and complications after them are treated by a doctor. The dispute therefore concerns who, after what training, may perform which procedures.

<Aside title="“Lex Szarlatan” is a different matter">

The Act of 31 July 2026 amending the Act on patients’ rights and the Patient Ombudsman, known as “Lex Szarlatan” (roughly, the “charlatan act”), concerns pseudo-medical practices, that is, presenting unproven methods as treatment. It does not decide which doctor may perform aesthetic procedures. On 19 August 2026 the president referred it to the Constitutional Tribunal for preventive review, so it cannot enter into force until the Tribunal rules.[^lex]

</Aside>

## The hypothesis: hours outside the NFZ

For aesthetic procedures the patient pays out of pocket, outside the NFZ, and the price is set by the clinic. If only a doctor may perform them, demand that cosmetologists used to serve may move to doctors. Hence the hypothesis: some doctors’ hours that could go to patients of the public system go to aesthetic clinics instead. A doctor who moves part of their work to where it pays better is doing nothing wrong. They are responding to conditions set by the rules.

This hypothesis has weak points:

- Hours in an aesthetic clinic may be added to work in the public system rather than replace it. Many doctors work in several places at once, which we describe in the mechanism of [multiple jobs](/en/mechanisms/multiple-jobs-parallel-contracts/).
- Some of the demand may not reach doctors at all, but disappear or move to the grey economy.
- Doctors performed aesthetic procedures before 2023 too, so it is not known what part of this work is new.
- The restriction covers doctors with a certificate or one of two specialisations. It is not known how many there are.

Doctors’ hours matter because the workforce is older than the headcount alone suggests. According to the medical chamber’s register, 164,892 doctors practise the profession, of whom 24,595 are aged 71 or over (as at 31.12.2025).[^crl] At the same time the workforce is not shrinking: according to the Ministry of Health, there were 155.8 thousand doctors with a licence to practise living in Poland in 2023 and 164.8 thousand in 2025.[^mz] How many doctors there are under different definitions, and why we do not combine these numbers in one calculation, we explain in our analysis [Why Poland is short of doctors](/en/analysis/why-poland-is-short-of-doctors/).

## The scale: estimates from one side of the dispute

The numbers on the size of the market and the possible outflow of doctors that circulate in the debate come from the Beauty Razem Foundation. The foundation is seeking the deletion of code 028, so it has its own interest in this dispute. That is why we give its numbers with the name of the source and with what is not known about them.

**Appeal to the prime minister of 10 November 2025.** The foundation writes that about 3,000 doctors perform aesthetic procedures today. Of about 141 thousand doctors working directly with patients, it says, 25–30% may leave for the aesthetic sector. It estimates additional NFZ costs at PLN 5–9 billion a year, and the state’s total losses at PLN 12–16 billion a year, including about <Money value={5850000000} scale="billion" decimals={2} /> of taxes and contributions from businesses that would close.[^br-apel] The figure of 141 thousand comes from Statistics Poland (GUS) and refers to 2024.[^gus] The foundation attributes the estimates of outflow and costs to its own analyses, but does not publish the calculation. The same appeal also contains a different range: “a risk of an outflow of 20–50 per cent of doctors” (our translation).[^br-apel]

**Survey of 13 November 2025.** The foundation surveyed 1,560 people from its own community online, so the sample was not random. 80.8% of respondents were cosmetologists with a degree. The survey is the source of the numbers on the industry itself: among respondents who perform procedures, 98.5% are women, and the average age of participants was 36. The 25–30% estimate does not come from this survey.[^br-raport] According to the foundation, about 70 thousand people perform aesthetic procedures, but it does not say how this number was counted.[^br-raport]

The order of magnitude is easy to check: 25–30% of 141 thousand is about 35–42 thousand doctors. That is more than ten times as many as, according to the same foundation, perform aesthetic procedures today. The range is close to another number from the survey: about 39 thousand people may lose their jobs in clinics (salon owners’ declarations extrapolated to the whole market), and the foundation’s president calls this a gap to be filled by doctors.[^br-raport] If this is the basis of the estimate, it assumes that every person who loses their job will be replaced by a doctor. Nobody has checked this.

<Aside variant="info" title="And leaving for abroad?">

There are no complete data on doctors leaving for abroad either. In its answer of 8 April 2026 to parliamentary question (interpellation) no. 15990, the Ministry of Health wrote that it has no detailed data on doctors leaving and returning. Nevertheless, it judges doctors’ emigration to be low.[^mz] There are three indirect measures, and each measures something different.

- **Medical chamber certificates** for the recognition of qualifications in another EU country. In 2019–2023 they were issued to 681, 584, 558, 887 and 664 doctors respectively (excluding dentists and certificates of equivalence of specialisation). The compilation does not cover later years.[^nil5] Issuing a certificate does not mean leaving. A longer series from the Supreme Audit Office (NIK) — 4,884 certificates from January 2018 to September 2022[^nik] — is discussed in our analysis [Who drops out of medicine and who emigrates](/en/analysis/medical-school-dropouts-and-emigration/).
- **OECD data.** In 2020/21, 16,275 Polish-born doctors were working in OECD countries. From this the OECD calculates an expatriation rate of 11.4% and notes that such rates should be read with caution.[^oecd-imo] This is a stock built up over whole careers, not the number of departures in a given year.
- **Germany.** At the end of 2025, 2,093 doctors with Polish citizenship were registered with German medical chambers, and 36 registered for the first time during 2025.[^baek] German statistics count citizenship, not the country where the doctor obtained the degree.

</Aside>

## What is missing to settle this

The hypothesis could be tested if two kinds of data existed. The first is the number of doctors who have obtained certificate 028, and the dates on which they obtained it. The second is information on how the work of the same doctors in the public system changed before and after they obtained the certificate.

The second part would be brought closer by [open pseudonymised data](/en/demand/), that is, the total paid to each person from public money, under a permanent identifier, without names. It would show whether payments to the same people rise or fall from year to year. A fall for some doctors would be the first hard signal that their hours are shifting outside the public system. Such data would not, however, show where those hours go.

This analysis is the basis for a point in our [wider programme](/program/#medycyna-estetyczna) (in Polish), in which we propose that the state should not strengthen this incentive by reserving aesthetic procedures for doctors.
