In Canada, treatment by a doctor and in hospital is paid for by the province’s public plan, and the patient pays nothing extra. Doctors, however, are not employees of the state — most of them bill the plan for every service. We describe how this system works according to Canadian acts and official data, and check what is public in it. The disputes between provincial governments and doctors over rates are described separately, in the text [on acts imposing terms on doctors](/en/analysis/canada-doctors-special-legislation/).

## Thirteen plans, one act

Each of the ten provinces and each of the three territories runs its own public insurance plan. The federal health act (*Canada Health Act*) sets five conditions for the plans: public administration, comprehensive coverage of necessary services, universality, validity across the whole country and accessibility without financial barriers. A province that meets them receives the federal transfer.[^cha]

About 71% of spending comes from public sources. Doctors account for 14.1% of the total, hospitals for 25.6% and medicines for 12.7%.[^nhex] The provincial plans do not cover medicines dispensed in pharmacies or dental treatment — these costs are borne by patients and private insurance policies. The federal government is filling this gap step by step: at the end of October 2026 the public dental care plan had 5,163,512 members.[^cdcp]

Canada has half as many deaths from treatable causes as Poland, with fewer doctors per inhabitant. The weak point is access: 9.1% of Canadians report unmet healthcare needs, and half are satisfied with the availability of good care where they live — against an OECD average of 64%.[^oecd-noty]

## Fee for service

Doctors bill the provincial plan as independent contractors, according to the province’s fee schedule. In 2024–25 the provinces paid them <Money value={40038101000} currency="CAD" scale="billion" decimals={2} /> in clinical payments: 70.4% as fee for service and 29.6% in alternative forms, such as lump-sum payments, sessional rates and salaries.[^cihi-platnosci]

The Auditor General of Ontario, who had previously examined this billing in 2016, reported in 2025 that the province had paid about 35,000 doctors <Money value={19500000000} currency="CAD" scale="billion" /> — against CAD 11.9 billion nine years earlier — and assessed the ministry’s oversight as reactive.[^ag-ontario]

## What the patient pays

For services covered by the plan a doctor may not charge the patient anything extra. The act enforces this financially: if charges were collected in a province, the federal government deducts the same amount from the transfer. In March 2025 the deductions came to <Money value={62204582} currency="CAD" />, of which CAD 35.2 million fell on Quebec and CAD 23.5 million on British Columbia — mainly for paid diagnostics and procedures in private clinics. A province recovers the money once it removes the cause; in the same month <Money value={51901885} currency="CAD" /> was reimbursed.[^cha]

## Doctors’ work and pay

The average gross clinical payment per doctor in 2024–25 was <Money value={429267} currency="CAD" />: <Money value={359577} currency="CAD" /> for family doctors and <Money value={501237} currency="CAD" /> for specialists. Between provinces the average ranges from <Money value={329835} currency="CAD" /> in Newfoundland and Labrador to <Money value={469212} currency="CAD" /> in Ontario.[^cihi-platnosci] This is not the doctor’s income: out of this amount the doctor pays for the surgery, staff and equipment, and the average also includes doctors working part-time.

## Public and private work at the same time

Canada separates the two sectors more sharply than European countries do. A doctor participating in the plan bills only the plan. A doctor may opt out and charge patients directly, but then the plan either does not pay for that doctor’s services or reimburses the patient at most the rate in the fee schedule. Outside Quebec only a handful do so: according to the federal report, in 2024–25 Ontario had 37,442 doctors in the plan and 10 outside it, and British Columbia 13,385 and 24. Quebec did not provide data.[^cha]

In Quebec there are more doctors working outside the plan, and in April 2025 the province restricted departures by an act. According to the government’s announcement, new doctors are to work in the public network for five years after graduating, and any doctor who wants to move to the private sector needs the consent of the public health agency.[^loi83] An organisation of doctors in private practice has challenged the act in court.[^loi83-skarga]

The ban on extra charges and on parallel practice was also the subject of a court dispute in British Columbia. Courts at two levels dismissed a private clinic’s claim, and on 6 April 2023 the Supreme Court of Canada refused to hear it.[^cambie]

## What is public

**Payments to doctors — by name in some provinces.** British Columbia publishes every year a list of payments from the plan to every provider who received at least <Money value={25000} currency="CAD" />; the edition for 2024/25 covers <Money value={8062362842} currency="CAD" scale="billion" decimals={2} />. An entry in the list may concern several doctors billing jointly, and the amounts are gross.[^bluebook] How the disclosure of payments works in other provinces, and what role it played in the disputes with doctors, is described [in a separate text](/en/analysis/canada-doctors-special-legislation/#disclosure-of-payments-in-these-disputes).

**Payments across the country — in aggregate.** The national health information institute (CIHI) publishes every year the totals and averages of payments by province and specialty.[^cihi-platnosci]

**Waiting times.** CIHI publishes the waiting time for selected procedures for each province, calculated under a single definition.[^cihi-kolejki]

**Deductions for charges to patients.** The annual report on the application of the act gives, for each province, the amount of the deductions and their cause, and for the provinces that provided data — the number of doctors working outside the plan.[^cha]

**What is not visible.** No list adds up the money that one doctor receives from different public sources — the provincial plan, the hospital and contracts — or shows income after deducting the costs of the practice.

## How long people wait

For several procedures there are common benchmarks: 182 days for a hip or knee replacement, 112 days for cataract surgery. Every year CIHI reports what share of patients were treated within the benchmark.[^cihi-kolejki] [^cihi-metoda]

In 2025 the median wait was 120 days for a hip replacement, 146 days for a knee replacement and 66 days for cataract surgery; one patient in ten waited more than 321 days for a hip. Radiotherapy started on time for 95.4% of patients.[^cihi-kolejki] The benchmark is not a guarantee — exceeding it does not give the patient the right to treatment elsewhere at the plan’s expense.

The second bottleneck is primary care. In 2024, 83% of adults had a regular doctor or another regular healthcare provider, which means about 5.7 million adults without such care.[^cihi-poz]

## Professional and criminal liability

**Professional.** The licence to practise is granted and withdrawn by the provincial medical colleges. In court disputes and before the colleges, doctors are supported by a medical protective association (CMPA), which has 119,308 member doctors. In 2025 it opened 10,280 new cases, including 6,076 before the colleges and 1,140 civil ones, and spent <Money value={259100000} currency="CAD" scale="million" /> on legal assistance.[^cmpa]

**Civil.** The patient pursues claims in court. Civil cases make up about one ninth of the CMPA’s new cases.[^cmpa]

**Criminal.** The Criminal Code provides for a penalty of up to life imprisonment for causing death by criminal negligence, and up to 10 years for causing bodily harm.[^kk] We found no statistics on such cases against doctors; the CMPA does not single them out in its report.[^cmpa]

Canada shows two things that Poland lacks. First, a common definition of waiting time: the same procedure is measured in the same way in every province and compared with the benchmark. Second, a clear line between work in the public plan and private practice — a doctor chooses one or the other, and most provinces report how many doctors are on each side.

## Method

We base the description on the federal health act and the annual report on its application, data from the national health information institute (CIHI), the report of the Auditor General of Ontario, British Columbia’s list of payments and the report of the doctors’ medical protective association. The spending for 2026 is a CIHI forecast, not settled amounts. The comparative indicators for Canada and Poland come from the OECD country notes, not from Eurostat.

We know the content of the Quebec act on work in the public sector from the provincial government’s announcement, not from the text of the act. We do not give the number of doctors working outside the plan in Quebec, because the federal report does not contain it.
