A patient harmed in a Polish hospital can take four routes today: apply to the Compensation Fund, sue the hospital, notify the prosecutor’s office, or complain to the disciplinary prosecutor at a medical chamber. The label **no-fault** appears in disputes about each of these routes, but each time it means something else.

Below: what the word means, how such systems work in countries that have had them for decades, what has been passed and what is proposed in Poland — and what would remain of a doctor’s liability if criminal liability for an unintentional error were removed.

## Three meanings of one word

In healthcare, no-fault names three separate arrangements. Each can be introduced without the others.

Abroad, no-fault usually means the first arrangement, sometimes together with the third. In the Polish debate the medical self-government uses the word mainly for the second.

## How it works in other countries

The oldest systems are those of the Nordic countries and New Zealand. They share one thing: the patient applies to a body that assesses the **harm**, not the person. They differ in when they pay.

The Swedish insurer Löf, owned by the regions, describes the rule as follows: compensation is due if the harm could have been avoided with care provided by an experienced specialist, and not merely because the treatment did not bring the expected result.[^lof] Denmark applies a similar test and in addition pays for rare and serious complications even when no one made an error.[^dk] The Norwegian act grants compensation when the harm results from a failure in the provision of care, “even if no one can be blamed”.[^no-lov]

These bodies do not link the number of applications to the number of errors. The Norwegian fund recorded a record 8,382 applications in 2025 and noted that it has no reason to believe treatment errors are increasing.[^npe]

In New Zealand the scheme replaced civil litigation: an injured person cannot sue for ordinary damages and is left only with a rarely used claim for exemplary damages. The author of a 2025 review reports that about 37% of treatment injury claims are declined.[^nz]

These systems are criticised in their own countries. The French fund ONIAM, which since 2002 has paid for serious medical accidents without fault, was assessed in 2017 by the French Court of Audit: it found the results “very far from the objectives”, the commissions had rejected three quarters of applications because of the severity threshold, and proceedings involving the fund took 2 years and 9 months on average.[^cdc]

## What remains of liability in those countries

In none of the countries described did the payment system replace the liability of the individual. Two tracks remained: criminal law and supervision of the profession.

**The criminal track.** In Sweden a doctor or nurse can be held liable for causing bodily harm or the death of a patient. Charges are rare, but they do occur and have ended in convictions, writes a Swedish scholar of medical law.[^wahlberg] In the United Kingdom, which did not introduce no-fault, 47 healthcare professionals, including 37 doctors, were prosecuted for gross negligence manslaughter between 1994 and 2018; 23 were convicted and 4 convictions were overturned on appeal.[^williams]

**Supervision of the profession.** In Sweden and Norway state bodies decide on the right to practise; in Denmark complaints about staff are handled by a state disciplinary board, independent of those who run healthcare.[^dk-disc] In New Zealand complaints go to the independent Health and Disability Commissioner, who received 3,477 of them in 2024/25.[^hdc]

This supervision is used. In {span}, authorities in Norway and Sweden revoked the right to practise **about {RATIO_ROUNDED} times more often** than Polish medical courts per number of doctors, and the British tribunal about {Math.round(RATIO_UK)} times more often. The systems differ in the range of cases, so this is an order of magnitude, not an exact measure; the calculation and caveats are in the analysis [The harshest penalty that is almost never imposed](/en/analysis/doctor-discipline-licence-revocation/).

## Poland: commissions, the quality act, the Fund

**Voivodeship commissions.** From 2012, voivodeship commissions ruled on whether a medical event had occurred. The Supreme Audit Office (NIK) examined eight of them and concluded in 2018 that proceedings before a commission “did not become a real alternative to court proceedings”. Applicants rejected 71.9% of the compensation offers made by hospitals, and the average length of first-instance proceedings ranged, depending on the commission, from 4 to 10.2 months.[^nik] The commissions were abolished in 2024.[^rpp2024]

**The quality act.** The government bill of December 2022 contained two no-fault elements. It prohibited reprisals against a person who reported an adverse event. It also allowed a court to apply extraordinary mitigation of the penalty to a doctor, nurse, midwife, paramedic or laboratory diagnostician for unintentionally exposing a patient to danger or unintentionally causing bodily harm — provided the perpetrator reported the event before law enforcement learned of it, was not under the influence of alcohol and did not breach the rules of caution intentionally or grossly.[^druk2898] That act fell: the Senate rejected it, and on 14 April 2023 the Sejm failed to gather the majority needed to override the Senate’s resolution.[^sejm2898] The act passed in June 2023, based on a bill tabled by deputies, contains no provisions on mitigating the penalty or on prohibiting reprisals against the reporter.[^ustawa-jakosc]

**The Medical Events Compensation Fund.** It has operated at the Patient Ombudsman since 6 September 2023 and is the Polish version of the first meaning of no-fault. It pays when an infection, bodily harm or the death of a patient “could with high probability have been avoided”, and only when the harm occurred in a hospital during services financed from public funds. The act sets amounts from <Money value={2000} /> to <Money value={200000} /> for a patient and from <Money value={20000} /> to <Money value={100000} /> for a relative after the patient’s death, indexed every year.[^fundusz]

In 2025 the Fund received **2,577 applications**. The Ombudsman issued 586 decisions granting a payment and 707 refusals. He paid out <Money value={34300000} scale="million" decimals={1} />, on average <Money value={58000} />.[^rpp2025] How to apply is described in the guide [Patient rights](/en/patient-rights/).

## What the medical self-government wants

In February 2024 the presidia of the Supreme Medical Council and the Supreme Council of Nurses and Midwives adopted a joint position: “The risk of criminal liability for unintentional acts resulting from the provision of health services should be lifted from persons practising medical professions. The patient, in turn, should be given mechanisms allowing effective, quick and out-of-court compensation for the harm suffered”.[^nrl] The self-government calls this demand the clause of the higher good.

The position concerns criminal liability; it does not mention civil or professional liability. According to the legal news service Prawo.pl, the proposal of the medical community is to add a provision to the Criminal Code: a person practising a medical profession would not be liable for an unintentional offence unless they were under the influence of alcohol or intoxicants, or the result stemmed from a gross failure to exercise caution.[^prawo] The self-government has not published the full wording of the provision in the documents we reached.

A similar provision has already been in force. The act of 28 October 2020 excluded the criminality of unintentional acts committed when diagnosing and treating COVID-19 during the state of epidemic, “unless the result caused was the outcome of a gross failure to exercise the caution required in the circumstances”.[^covid]

On 2 June 2025 the minister of health appointed a team which a month later was named the Team for Legal Solutions on the Civil and Criminal Liability of Doctors and Dentists. It is to draw up a concept of provisions “serving to strengthen patient safety” and to reduce adverse events. The order sets no deadline and binds the members to confidentiality.[^zespol] By 17 November 2026 we had found no published report of the team and no bill with such a clause.

In {PENALTIES.period}, medical courts imposed {formatEN(PENALTIES_TOTAL)} penalties at first instance. **{mildPct}% of them were an admonition, a reprimand or a fine.** In {allYears}, {plTotal} doctors finally lost the right to practise.[^nil-sady] Doctors are judged there by doctors elected by assemblies of doctors, and a case leaves the self-government only with a cassation appeal to the Supreme Court — as described in [How Poland’s medical courts work](/en/mechanisms/how-medical-courts-work/).

A civil lawsuit concerns the facility, not the person. The Ministry of Justice keeps records of cases for damages “for harm caused by the health service” and divides them by defendant: a public healthcare facility, the State Treasury or local government, a non-public facility. In 2024, **{CIVIL_2024.filed} such cases** were filed with district and regional courts. First-instance courts disposed of {CIVIL_2024.closed}: in {CIVIL_2024.granted} ({civilGrantedPct}%) they upheld the claim in whole or in part, they dismissed {CIVIL_2024.dismissed}, and {CIVIL_2024.settled} ended in a settlement. At the end of the year {formatEN(CIVIL_2024.pending)} cases were pending — more than three times as many as the courts disposed of during the year.[^ms-cywilne]

## Arguments in favour

Criminal liability for an error is already rare today, and proceedings are long. In 2017, prosecutors’ offices conducted {formatEN(PK_2017.cases)} proceedings concerning medical errors. They closed {formatEN(PK_2017.decisions)} with a decision on the merits: {PK_2017.indictments} with an indictment, {PK_2017.pleas} with a motion for voluntary submission to a penalty, {formatEN(PK_2017.discontinued)} by discontinuation and {PK_2017.refusals} by refusing to open proceedings. Discontinuations and refusals made up **{pkClosedPct}% of decisions on the merits**.[^pk] The National Prosecutor’s Office has not published newer data in this form.

The medical self-government concludes from this that criminal proceedings burden many doctors but rarely end in a judgment, and that fear of them discourages the disclosure of errors. A Swedish legal analysis reaches a similar assessment: punishing a mistake does little to prevent the next ones, and the risk of prosecution lowers the willingness to report events.[^wahlberg] The self-government’s demand also keeps a gross failure to exercise caution punishable.

We found no study showing that payments without deciding on fault worsen patient safety.
