The Netherlands has no public payer in the Polish sense. Every inhabitant must buy a policy from one of the private insurers, and these buy treatment from hospitals and doctors. The state determines what the policy covers and enforces the rules. We describe how this system works according to Dutch acts and official data, and check what is public in it.

## Private insurers, public rules

The Health Insurance Act, in force since 2006, imposes a duty on both sides. An inhabitant must take out insurance, and an insurer must conclude a contract with anyone who applies — regardless of age and state of health.[^zvw] The scope of the basic policy is the same for everyone and is determined by the state.[^profil]

The premium paid to the insurer covers about half of the costs. The other half is financed by an income-related contribution, collected by the tax office and divided among the insurers so as to even out differences in the health of their customers. The state pays for children.[^profil] People on low incomes receive a subsidy towards the premium.[^profil]

The market is concentrated: in 2026 all insurers belong to ten groups, and the four largest serve 84.7% of the insured.[^vektis]

## What it costs

Although the insurers are private, Eurostat counts compulsory contributions as public sources — hence the public share of 83%.

## Contracts instead of a fee schedule

The insurers negotiate prices and volumes with each hospital. Hospitals are usually not-for-profit foundations. Specialist doctors are tied to them by two kinds of arrangement: some work on their own account and contract with the hospital through a specialists’ company, and the rest — mainly in university hospitals — are salaried.[^profil] According to the statistical office CBS, in 2024, of 20,900 specialists (excluding psychiatrists), 54% had income solely from an employment contract, 29% worked through their own company and 11% were self-employed.[^cbs]

A family doctor receives a fixed fee for every registered patient and separately for consultations. In 2025 the basic financing segment, made up mainly of these two items, accounted for 70% of the costs billed in the three segments; primary care as a whole cost €5.47 billion. 17,025,000 insured people were registered with family doctors.[^nza-huisarts]

## What the patient pays

Apart from the premium, an adult covers the first €385 of treatment costs in the year. They can voluntarily raise this threshold in exchange for a lower premium.[^zvw] [^premie] Dental treatment for adults is not part of the basic package.[^profil] Out-of-pocket payments by patients account for 11.9% of health spending — less than the EU average.

## Doctors’ work and pay

The statistical office publishes specialists’ incomes separately for three groups. Salaried specialists had an average annual wage of €190,100 in 2024 (provisional data, not converted to full-time equivalents). Self-employed specialists — 2,200 people — made an average profit of €182,200 in 2023. The third group, 5,700 specialists working through their own company, had on average €137,400 of pay and €47,700 of dividends in 2023.[^cbs] The statistics do not show the profit that remained in the company.

## Public and private work at the same time

The division into a public and a private sector has a different meaning in the Netherlands than in Poland: the hospitals and insurers are private, while the rules and most of the money are public. The equivalent of the Polish question is whether a hospital specialist also works in an independent clinic that competes for the same patients. We found no public register of such occupations, nor the number of doctors working in both places.

## What is public

**Board members’ pay — by name.** The act on the standardisation of top incomes covers institutions financed from public funds, including hospitals. In its annual report an institution must give the pay of every member of its management by first name and surname, and the report is public. The cap for 2026 is €262,000, and for smaller institutions correspondingly less — from €147,000.[^wnt] [^wnt-kwoty] [^wnt-zorg] The institution also discloses employees who earn more than the cap. The provision concerns management; it does not cover self-employed doctors.

**Institutions’ reports.** Healthcare institutions file a report every year in a central system.[^digimv]

**Payments from industry.** The financial ties of pharmaceutical and medical companies with doctors and institutions are collected by a central register; almost €97 million was reported in it for 2025.[^transparantie]

**Waiting times.** Since August 2021 hospitals have reported waiting times to the healthcare regulator (NZa) every two weeks, and each hospital gives them on its website for each specialty.[^nza-toegang] [^wachttijden]

**What is not visible.** The profit a specialist leaves in their company, insurers’ payments to individual doctors, and hospital doctors’ secondary occupations.

## How many doctors there are and how long people wait

<CountryBars title="Doctors and unmet healthcare needs (Eurostat)" codes={['NL', 'PL', 'EU']} keys={['doctors', 'unmet', 'unmetWait']} />

The Netherlands has roughly as many doctors per inhabitant as Poland, and 0.5% of people there report unmet healthcare needs, against an EU average of 2.4%.

The insurer is responsible for access. If a patient waits too long, they can ask the insurer to point them to another institution. In mental healthcare there are agreed standards: four weeks to the first interview and ten weeks to the start of treatment; once these are exceeded, the insurer is to propose another institution or reimburse the cost of treatment outside its network.[^wachttijden] The standards are, however, widely exceeded: in 2024 about 67% of people referred to specialist mental healthcare waited longer than four weeks, and nearly 40% longer than ten weeks for treatment.[^profil] The regulator said in July 2025 that the standard was exceeded in all diagnosis groups.[^nza-ggz]

The number of places in medical studies and in specialist training is set by the government on the advice of an independent workforce planning body. In December 2025 that body recommended 3,330 places in medicine a year, against 2,984 currently financed. In May 2026 the government adopted the recommendations for family doctors (1,028 places) and specialists (1,320 places in 2027), and has not yet decided on the number of places in medicine.[^capaciteit]

## Professional and criminal liability

**Professional.** Complaints against doctors and other healthcare professionals are heard by regional disciplinary tribunals, and appeals by the central tribunal. In 2025 the regional tribunals received 1,262 complaints. After a hearing 190 were found to be justified: 79 warnings, 45 reprimands, 16 suspensions and 14 removals from the register were imposed. The central tribunal upheld 78.3% of the decisions appealed against.[^tucht] The figures cover all healthcare professions, not only doctors.

**Civil.** Every institution must belong to a recognised disputes committee, which can award the patient compensation of up to at least €25,000 without court proceedings.[^wkkgz]

**Criminal.** The criminal code provides for up to two years’ imprisonment for causing death by negligence, and up to four in the case of recklessness; for causing grievous bodily harm by negligence — up to one year, and up to two years in the case of recklessness. If the act was committed in the exercise of a profession, the court may increase the sentence by one third and ban the person from practising the profession.[^sr] According to the prosecution service’s guidelines, the yardstick in medical cases is gross negligence, and before deciding to prosecute the prosecutor seeks the opinion of a specialised centre.[^om] We found no figure for the number of such cases.

The Dutch example shows the limit of transparency based on the form of employment. The cap and publication by name cover boards and salaried employees, but not doctors who do the same work through a company. In Poland the same dividing line runs between the employment contract and the civil-law (B2B) contract.

## Method

We base the description on the texts of Dutch acts from the official legal database, documents of the government and parliament, data from the statistical office (CBS) and the healthcare regulator (NZa), the report of the disciplinary tribunals, and the profile of the Netherlands prepared by the OECD and the European Observatory for the European Commission. The comparative indicators for the Netherlands, Poland and the EU come from the same Eurostat datasets and the same year.

We give specialists’ incomes as reported by the statistical office, as annual averages not converted to full-time equivalents; for doctors working through their own companies they comprise pay and dividends, without the profit retained in the company. The data on the concentration of insurers come from an institute run by the industry itself.
