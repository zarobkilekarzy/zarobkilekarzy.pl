There is no single answer to the question of how many Poles are satisfied with their healthcare. Four surveys from 2025–2026 give **19%, 25%, 35% and 38%**.[^iris][^cbos][^ipsos][^stada] The differences arise because each survey asks a different question of a different group of people. The conclusion is nevertheless shared: clearly fewer than half of respondents give a good rating, and in international comparisons Poland is near the bottom of the list.

## Four questions, four numbers

The lowest result comes from the most sharply worded question. IRIS Network, a network of research agencies, asks respondents to choose one of four descriptions of the system: “excellent”, “basically good”, “deteriorating” or “in a state of crisis”. In Poland 19% choose one of the first two.[^iris] CBOS simply asks about satisfaction with the way healthcare currently works in the country and gets 25%.[^cbos]

The highest results come from online surveys. The Ipsos index is an average of five questions, one of which concerns easy access to a pharmacy, which is usually rated better than access to a doctor.[^ipsos] STADA asks about satisfaction with the public healthcare system; the report does not quote the exact wording of the question.[^stada]

The online samples number a few hundred people, so a single result has a wide margin. For a sample of 500 Ipsos gives a credibility interval of ±5 percentage points.[^ipsos] The difference between 35% and 38% falls within that margin; the difference between Poland and a survey’s average does not.

## CBOS: the satisfied have been a minority since 2001

CBOS has been asking the same question for a quarter of a century, so only this survey shows change over time. In June 2025, 25% of respondents were satisfied and 70% dissatisfied. Two years earlier it was 27% and 70%.[^cbos]

In fourteen measurements since 2001 the satisfied have never been a majority: there were most of them in March 2007 (37%) and fewest in February 2012 (19%). The two lines have run almost parallel for a quarter of a century, and the gap between them has never fallen below 21 percentage points.

The overall rating is made up of very different partial ratings. Access to specialist doctors comes out worst: **82%** of respondents rate it badly. The possibility of booking an appointment at a convenient time is rated badly by 63%. Doctors’ competence is rated best, appreciated by **73%** of respondents.[^cbos] The dissatisfaction therefore concerns mainly access to treatment, not the way doctors treat.

Where the waiting lists come from is described in our analyses [Why Poland is short of doctors](/en/analysis/why-poland-is-short-of-doctors/) and [Private visits: the longer the queue, the higher the price](/en/analysis/private-visits-queues-and-prices/).

The rating also depends on age. Among people aged 65 and over, 36% are satisfied, 11 percentage points more than in the whole sample.[^cbos]

## Poland among other countries

In the IRIS Network survey Poland ranks 19th of 20 countries. Only Turkey has a lower result (15%), and the average of all countries is 45%.[^iris] The report also gives what respondents consider the main cause of the problems: in Poland 70% point to inefficient management and 30% to insufficient funding.

In the STADA survey 38% of people in Poland are satisfied with the public system, against an average of 56%. A year earlier the average was 58%.[^stada] The international report does not contain a full table: it gives the results of 15 of 20 countries, and Slovakia has the same result as Poland. Poland’s seventeenth place is given only in the company’s Polish press release.[^stadapl]

The same survey asked what respondents would change first if they were health minister. In Poland 66% pointed to shorter waiting times and more staff, and 57% to easier access to primary care.[^stada] It is the same picture as in the partial ratings from CBOS.

<Aside title="The limits of these data" variant="note">
- We take the IRIS result for Poland from the network’s report: **19%**. A summary in the daily Rzeczpospolita gives 22%, with components of 4% and 16%, which add up to 20%.[^rp] None of those numbers is in the report, so we do not use them.
- The surveys measure opinions, not the quality of treatment. People who did not use healthcare in the past year also answer.
- Comparisons between countries depend on respondents’ expectations, not only on how the system works. The set of countries in the IRIS survey changes between waves, so the averages from successive years cannot be compared directly.
- STADA is a pharmaceutical company and its report is promotional material; Ipsos and STADA survey online panels, not random samples.
</Aside>
