A queue for the doctor exists in every public healthcare system. Countries differ in whether they count it, to whom they show the result and what they promise the patient who waits too long. We checked this in fifteen countries. We do not rank the countries by the length of their queues — national statistics measure different things and cannot be set side by side. We compare the measurement itself.

## Three questions for every country

For every country we established whether there is an official measurement and how detailed it is, whether the law sets a maximum waiting time, and what happens when that deadline passes.

## Countries with a deadline that means something

**Denmark.** The region has one month for diagnosis and then one month to start treatment. If it misses the deadline, the patient can choose a private hospital or clinic with which the regions have an agreement, and the region pays the bill.[^dk-ustawa] In 2024 the right to diagnosis on time was met in 79% of cases of somatic illness; the average wait for surgery was 57 days.[^dk-maal]

**Sweden.** The guarantee laid down in an act and a regulation counts successive stages: contact with a primary care centre on the same day, a medical assessment within three days, a visit to a specialist within 90 days and a procedure within a further 90. When a region misses the deadline for a specialist visit or a procedure, it has to arrange treatment with another provider at no additional cost to the patient.[^se-hsl] In September 2026, 74% of first specialist visits and 65% of procedures took place on time.[^se-soc]

In both countries the cost of the delay is therefore borne by whoever organises the treatment, not by the patient. A similar right is laid down in England: the constitution of the public health service gives the patient the right to treatment within a maximum waiting time and, where that is not possible, the right for the health service to take all reasonable steps to offer alternative providers.[^uk-konstytucja]

## Countries that count and publish

**England** publishes the most detailed data: every month, for every hospital and specialty. In September 2026 there were 7.4 million referrals waiting for planned treatment, and 65.8% were within 18 weeks — against a standard of 92%. There were 116,353 cases waiting more than a year.[^uk-rtt]

**Spain** announces its waiting lists every six months for each autonomous community. At the end of 2025 there were 853,740 people waiting for surgery, 122 days on average — from 50 days in Madrid to 173 in Andalusia. The ministry notes that it only compiles the regions’ data and that the communities are responsible for their reliability. For five procedures, including hip and knee replacement and cataract surgery, a 180-day guarantee applies.[^es-sisle]

**Canada** compares every province against target times; the tables start in 2008. In 2025, 69.2% of patients received a hip replacement within the 182-day target, and the median was 120 days.[^ca-cihi]

**Italy** launched a national platform in 2025. According to its data, in the first half of 2026 the first appointment offered was within the norm for 78.5% of visits. The platform measures the first available appointment, not the actual waiting time, covers about one fifth of services and does not show individual facilities.[^it-ocpi]

**Hungary** keeps a register in which patients find their own place on the list by an identifier, without names.[^hu-ebtv] The register covers 15 types of procedure.[^hu-287]

**The Netherlands** shifts the responsibility to the insurer: hospitals report waiting times every two weeks, and a patient who waits too long can demand to be directed to another facility.[^nl-nza] [^nl-rijk] In mental health care the agreed norms are exceeded in all diagnostic groups.[^nl-ggz]

## Countries that do not count

In **Germany** there is no official measurement. The act obliges the appointment service centres to offer a specialist appointment within four weeks;[^de-sgb] we found no official statistics on actual waiting times.

In **France** the only source is a survey by the ministry’s statistics office published in 2018: half of patients waited up to two days for a visit to a family doctor and up to 52 days for a visit to an ophthalmologist.[^fr-drees]

In **Belgium** the state centre of expertise found in 2024 that nobody measures waiting times systematically, and recommended setting up a body that would collect the data and set maximum thresholds.[^be-kce]

**Czechia** has deadlines without measurement: a regulation of 2012 sets 52 weeks for hip and knee replacement and 30 weeks for cataract surgery, but there is no nationwide monitoring to check whether they are met.[^cz-307] [^cz-profil]

In **Austria** the act requires the federal states to keep waiting lists for planned operations where the wait exceeds four weeks.[^at-kakug] Upper Austria publishes the waiting time for every hospital: in September 2026 the wait for a hip replacement ranged from 10 to 90 weeks, depending on the facility.[^at-ooe] The Austrian audit office found that at the end of 2024 there was also no nationwide monitoring of waiting times for a doctor’s appointment, although it had recommended one in 2021.[^at-rh]

In the **USA** we found no national official measurement.

## Poland: there is measurement, there is no deadline

The Polish act imposes a duty to measure. The provider keeps a waiting list and on every working day reports the first available appointment to the National Health Fund (NFZ), and the Fund publishes, at least once a month, the number of people waiting and the average waiting time for every facility.[^pl-ustawa] Anyone can check the first available appointment at a chosen clinic or hospital on the NFZ website.[^pl-terminy]

The act does not, however, set a general maximum waiting time — the NFZ only informs the patient about the possibility of treatment at other facilities. The exception is the “permissible waiting time” set individually when a patient applies for consent to treatment abroad.[^pl-ustawa]

The reliability of the lists raises doubts. In 2024 the Supreme Audit Office (NIK) examined specialist outpatient clinics for children: at eight of the nine providers audited (89%) the admission schedules were incomplete or contained errors.[^pl-nik]

## Why the figures cannot be compared directly

The OECD collects from countries the waiting time for seven planned procedures under two definitions. Both count the time from entry on the list after assessment by a specialist. The first measures how long the people who are still on the list on the day of measurement have been waiting. The second — how long the people already operated on had waited. Poland reports both, and in 2024 the difference between them was fivefold.[^oecd]

Both Polish figures concern the same operation and the same year. The first covers everyone who is on the list on the day of measurement, including those waiting a very long time; the second — only those who got their procedure. It is similar for knee replacement: 476 days on the list and 109 days for those operated on.[^oecd] One of these figures given without its definition may suggest that Polish queues are the longest, and the other — that they are among the shorter ones.

Two caveats. The Polish data cover only primary operations, without replacement of an existing implant.[^oecd-meta] The OECD also notes that waiting times in Poland fell markedly compared with 2019, after the NFZ increased its funding of these procedures.[^oecd-rozdzial] In the data for 2025 the medians are 282 days on the list and 91 days for those operated on.[^oecd]

For the same reason we do not set national data side by side: England counts referrals, not people; Canada counts the time from the moment the patient is ready for the procedure; Italy measures the first available appointment.

Poland is not among the countries that do not count their queues — the duty to measure is in the act, and the data are published for every facility. It differs from Denmark and Sweden in that little follows from the measurement for the patient: there is no general deadline after which the system must pay for treatment elsewhere. The second issue is reliability: the NIK audit of clinics for children showed errors at eight of the nine providers examined. We describe the mechanism in the text on [restricted access to the profession](/en/mechanisms/restricted-access-to-the-profession/).

## Method

For every country we checked who measures waiting times, how often and in how much detail the results are published, whether the law sets a maximum waiting time and what the consequence of exceeding it is. We rely on the texts of acts, the publications of public authorities and the reports of audit bodies; the figures for Hungary and Italy are known to us from studies citing official data. We give each country’s figures in its own definitions and do not set them against one another.

The only comparable source is the OECD dataset from which the chart is drawn; Belgium, France, Czechia, Germany, Austria and the USA do not report data to it. The chart shows 2024 because that is the year for which the most countries have data; we give the more recent values for Poland in the text. We describe the countries at greater length in the texts collected on the page [Healthcare in other countries](/en/countries/).
