In a single month the same doctor may bill under contracts in several public facilities. Each knows only its own part — its own hours and its own invoices. Nobody adds them together: neither the hours nor the money.

For years not even AOTMiT (the Agency for Health Technology Assessment and Tariff System), the agency that collects pay data in healthcare, did so. As it admitted in an interview with the daily Rzeczpospolita,[^aotmit] it saw earnings only within a single facility: a doctor working in four places was, for the agency, four unconnected entries. Menedżer Zdrowia, a health-management magazine, has also described why this happens.[^menedzer]

Since 5 August 2026 the minister of health may request from facilities pay data linked to the PESEL number (the Polish personal identification number) and the number of the licence to practise (PWZ), so one person’s payments can now be added up. Facilities, however, hand them over only at the minister’s request, and the act does not provide for their publication.[^ustawa-972] We discuss the provisions in our analysis [The state will count, the citizen will not see](/en/analysis/pay-data-act-vs-public-register/).

## Three conditions the Polish system meets

Working in several places is not in itself an abuse. The problem is that the total cannot be seen. For one person to combine facilities without a limit and without anyone knowing, three things must happen at once — in Poland all of them do.

1. **A contract instead of employment.** Employment is limited by the Labour Code: 48 hours a week, up to about 78 with an opt-out clause. A contract — B2B or a civil-law contract — is outside these limits, and about 73% of specialists work this way. More in our text [Contract or employment](/en/mechanisms/contract-vs-employment/).
2. **No common register.** No institution compiles one person’s hours or payments from different workplaces. The total for one doctor does not exist in any public database — since 5 August 2026 the minister may calculate it from data requested from facilities, but does not have to publish it ([analysis of the act](/en/analysis/pay-data-act-vs-public-register/)). What we propose instead is set out in [our demand](/en/demand/).
3. **A shortage of doctors.** In specialties with shortages, directors compete for anyone who will cover an on-call shift, and do not ask where else that person works or how many hours they already have. Staff shortages do not curb the phenomenon; they drive it. More in our analysis [Why Poland is short of doctors](/en/analysis/why-poland-is-short-of-doctors/).

## The provision that allows it, and its expiry date

Record rotas do not come from a gap in the law but from a specific provision. Article 96 of the Act on medical activity, the opt-out clause, allows an employee with higher medical education who works in a 24-hour hospital and performs on-call duty to agree in writing to work more than an average of 48 hours a week. The provision will expire on 1 January 2028. That date was written into a 2018 amendment, not the current reform.

The opt-out, however, applies only to employment — it does not cover civil-law contracts or self-employment. The limit therefore hits the form of employment that is visible in the documents anyway, and bypasses the one whose totals nobody counts. On the employment side the effect will be felt. A doctor who today works an average of 260 hours a month would have to cut about 52 hours, mainly at the expense of the best-paid night and holiday on-call shifts, and hospitals estimate that they will need about 25% more full-time posts, Rynek Zdrowia reported.[^opt-out]

### Standby “on the phone”

Employment has one more paid form of availability that does not require being in the hospital. The employer may oblige a doctor to remain on standby to provide services: the doctor is reachable by phone and comes in when called. For each hour of such standby the doctor is entitled to half the hourly rate of base pay, and when called in, the work is settled as on-call duty — as provided in Article 98 of the Act on medical activity.[^art-98]

Standby in itself is not an abuse. But since nobody compiles one person’s hours, it is also impossible to see whether standby in one facility overlaps with work or standby in another.

<Aside title="A new limit: no more than two full-time posts in total">

The first limit that refers directly to work in many places is contained in a Ministry of Health bill published in the Government Legislation Centre (RCL) on 24 July 2026. Total working time with all employers could not exceed two full-time posts. In each place one would need at least a half-time post, and any further employment would require the consent of the first healthcare provider. The bill also introduces a monthly pay ceiling — 16 times the minimum wage for two full-time posts — and a contract rate of about <Money value={240} /> an hour, Rynek Zdrowia reported.[^rz-projekt] The government adopted the bill on 22 September 2026, with half a year to adjust existing contracts.[^rz-rzad] On 7 October the first reading took place in the Sejm Health Committee,[^rz-komisja] and on 22 October the Sejm passed the act; it is now before the Senate.[^glosowanie] We describe what the adopted text contains in our analysis [Pay limits for medical staff](/en/analysis/medical-pay-limits-act/).

Two caveats. The limit is to cover only entities with an NFZ contract, so private work stays outside the calculation. And since there is no identifier by which one person’s settlements can be added up, enforcement will rest on employers’ declarations.

</Aside>

## A case from 2026: 331 hours a month

The mechanism is best seen in a case the media described in 2026. It is not about the size of the rate, but about the fact that no register added up the hours or payments of one person spread over several hospitals.

A Warsaw doctor — a resident doctor without a completed specialisation who coordinated a hospital emergency department (SOR) — worked simultaneously in four public facilities. In 2025 the doctor earned more than <Money value={1600000} scale="million" /> from self-employment, according to the doctor’s asset declaration.[^tvn] According to data from the hospital where the doctor coordinated the SOR alone, 3,976 hours of work were reported there over the year, an average of 331 hours a month.[^tvn][^rz-331] On top of that, for four months of 2025 the doctor had a contract in another Warsaw hospital and reported 732 hours there, an average of 183 a month, Rzeczpospolita reported.[^rp-732] The case was also covered by Polsat News[^polsat] and Interia.[^interia]

That was not the full picture. During the affair it emerged that since February 2026 the same doctor had also been deputy coordinator of the SOR in a fifth Warsaw hospital. In March alone 290 hours were reported there, for which the doctor was owed more than <Money value={104000} /> (<Money value={350} /> an hour, in some cases <Money value={400} />). Regardless of the hours, the doctor was entitled to a function allowance of <Money value={25000} /> a month, Polsat News reported.[^piaty-szpital] If these hours were added to the average from the hospital where the doctor coordinated the SOR alone, the result would be about 620 hours a month — more than 20 hours a day, every day, and that without the other facilities. This is an illustrative calculation, because the average refers to 2025 and the 290 hours to March 2026. Nobody added these hours up.

In June 2026 the doctor gave up the seat of district councillor and returned about <Money value={500000} scale="million" /> to the hospital.[^mandat] On 6 October the doctor was charged with fraud involving property of significant value: according to the prosecutor’s office, the doctor submitted unreliable invoices for more than <Money value={583000} />. The prosecutor’s office announced this a day later. The doctor did not plead guilty, and a charge is not a verdict.[^zarzut-pap][^zarzut-rmf][^zarzut-bankier]

<Aside title="A resident doctor, not a specialist">

The doctor in this case was a resident doctor, that is, a doctor in specialist training. Nevertheless, the doctor coordinated the SOR and — according to accounts of staff described by Do Rzeczy[^do-rzeczy] and wPolityce[^wpolityce] — took the most serious cases from the “red zone”, which a resident should not handle independently; others were said to have corrected the doctor’s mistakes. The doctor received the licence to practise on 6 November 2024,[^pwz] and the team organising the department’s work was set up a few months earlier.

This is the second layer of the same gap: nobody checks together either the hours and payments or whether a given person is qualified for what they do. Witness accounts are not final findings, and a prosecutor’s charge is not a verdict — the case is being examined by the prosecutor’s office and the medical chamber. We deliberately do not give the name: we describe the mechanism, not the person.

</Aside>

### Hours that do not exist

At the county hospital in Braniewo (Warmian-Masurian region), one doctor under contract was at times paid for more than 1,000 hours in a month that has at most 744. The doctor did not need several facilities for this: the same hours were billed under two or three headings at once, for example as on-call duty in an ambulance and as cover for a ward.

There were days billed as 72 hours.[^braniewo] Payments reached <Money value={1772879.5} decimals={2} />. The hospital estimates the loss over three years at about <Money value={3800000} scale="million" /> — this is an internal calculation prepared by the hospital’s lawyer and accountant, not a finding of investigators.[^de-braniewo] It was only discovered by the new management board during a review of payments. The doctor handed in a notice of termination and issued the last invoice in July 2025.[^de-braniewo] The investigation opened on 2 March 2026 concerns the actions of the former president of the company running the hospital, not the doctor.[^prok-elblag] By July nobody had been charged,[^de-braniewo] and on 15 September 2026 the prosecutor’s office suspended the investigation pending an expert opinion.[^tvn-braniewo]

Braniewo is not an exception:

- At the Sosnowiec Municipal Hospital a surgeon reported about 4,812 hours in 2023 — more than 13 hours a day for the whole year, including weekends and holidays. After the case came to light, the hospital itself introduced a limit of 350 hours a month.[^sosnowiec]
- In Tomaszów Lubelski the hospital director, who is also a doctor, reported 491 hours in one month, of which 351 as a doctor. The National Labour Inspectorate (PIP) took up the case.[^tomaszow]
- The longest single on-call shift documented by the PIP lasted almost 176 hours — more than a week without a break. Another doctor accumulated more than 1,200 hours of overtime.[^pip]

Each time, the limit was set only by an inspection or by the hospital itself, not by the law.

## A public case: the same arithmetic, only visible

Not every such case means abuse — and the next one shows well why. The director of the county hospital in Łosice (Mazowsze, about 7,000 inhabitants) reported in her asset declaration for 2025 about <Money value={1700000} scale="million" /> gross from work: about <Money value={437000} /> for running the hospital and about <Money value={1300000} scale="million" /> from on-call duty as a doctor, plus about <Money value={111000} /> of pension. This was described by Wirtualna Polska,[^losice-wp] Money.pl[^losice-money] and TVP Warszawa.[^losice-tvp] She is 72 years old, a paediatrician and neonatologist. She takes on-call shifts because — as she explained on Radio dla Ciebie[^losice-rdc] — otherwise there would be nobody to staff the children’s ward. Her rate is the same as that of other doctors in this hospital: <Money value={205} /> an hour.

Let us do the calculation: **PLN 1.3 million ÷ PLN 205/h ≈ 6,300 hours a year**, an average of about 17 hours a day, without a day off — alongside working as director until 15:35. If some of the on-call shifts were paid at a higher rate, there would be fewer hours, but the workload would still be hard to imagine. This is our calculation from the declared amount and the rate, not an official number of hours.

And most importantly: this is not an accusation. The case is public and declared, and according to the director, it keeps the ward going. It can be seen, however, only because a hospital director must file an asset declaration. A doctor on an ordinary contract — and about 73% of specialists work this way — files none. The same total would be just as real, but nobody would see it. Transparency here is not a punishment, but the only difference between a case we know about and those we do not. We do not give the name, although this is a public figure: we describe the mechanism and its transparency.

## “Travelling doctors”: the opposite movement, the same gap

The same invisibility has another side. Instead of collecting hours in many places, some specialists do the opposite: in each facility they take only what pays best — they perform a well-priced procedure and move on, without on-call duty, complications or “expensive” patients. The director of the hospital in Płońsk calls them travelling doctors (*lekarze objazdowi*); the media call them tourists.

In an interview with Wirtualna Polska[^plonsk] he described a hospital in which one of three surgeons is on call without a break, while the contract is filled by visiting orthopaedists and neurosurgeons. Everyone is satisfied: the hospital fulfils its contract without treating costly cases, and the visitors earn well and do not have to take on-call shifts. His summary: “They leave the patient and drive off” (our translation).

This is above all a rational response to a badly designed system, not the doctors’ fault. It is driven by two things: a contract without an obligation to take on-call duty ([contract or employment](/en/mechanisms/contract-vs-employment/)) and pricing in which some procedures tend to be overvalued, while treating complications and difficult patients is undervalued ([pricing of services](/en/mechanisms/pricing-of-healthcare-services/)).

Lack of transparency, however, plays a specific role here: nobody sees the whole pattern. Each facility knows its own contract and its own procedure. That the same person collects well-paid procedures in five counties and nowhere takes an on-call shift does not follow from any database. Compiling one person’s settlements would show this pattern immediately: a high total, many facilities, zero on-call shifts.

There is a bitter paradox in this. A doctor who stays on call and takes on complications looks less productive in the documents and usually earns less than a colleague who only performs procedures. “Tourist” is, in any case, a media shorthand — many commuting doctors fill real staff gaps.
