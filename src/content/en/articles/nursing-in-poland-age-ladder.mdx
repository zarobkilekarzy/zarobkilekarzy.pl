The Central Register of Nurses and Midwives (CRPiP) lists 333,336 nurses and 43,965 midwives.[^crpip] We set data on their age, the number in work and the inflow into the profession against the shortage forecasts that the professional self-government has published for years, and check what those forecasts imply today. The figures come mainly from the register and reports of the Supreme Chamber of Nurses and Midwives (NIPiP), from the OECD and from Statistics Poland (GUS). We give the date next to each figure, because the age data and the forecasts come from different years.

## The age ladder

In January 2023 the register held 315,670 nurses. The largest group were people aged 51–60 (31.5%), the second largest those aged 61–70 (25.3%). 34% of registered nurses were over 60, and 16.5% were under 40.[^nipip2023] We did not find a newer age distribution in NIPiP reports.

Midwives are younger: just under one in three (almost 30%) was of retirement age, and 25.7% of them were under 40.[^nipip2023]

The register is not the same as the staff at the bedside: about 74% of registered nurses work.[^nipip2023] NIPiP gave the age of employed nurses alone in its 2022 report. At that time 36.0% of them were aged 51–60, and 29.4% were over 60.[^nipip2022]

The average age of a registered nurse rose from 44.2 years in 2008 to 55.2 years at the end of 2025, and that of a midwife from 43.2 to 52.2 years. The average is, however, rising ever more slowly: in 2022 it went up by 0.46 years, in 2025 by 0.28 years.[^crpip] This coincides with the growing inflow into the profession, which we describe below.

## How many nurses are of retirement age

Two figures circulate in the debate: 34% of nurses “already of retirement age” and 65% “by 2030”. They describe different groups in different years, so they should not be combined into one series.

**34% is a measurement.** That is the share of registered nurses who were 61 or older in January 2023.[^nipip2023] Women, who make up 96.5% of the profession,[^crpip] become entitled to a pension at 60, and 60-year-olds fall into the 51–60 group in the chart. If the single-year cohorts in this group are roughly equal, about {RETIREMENT_NOW}% of registered nurses were of retirement age at that time.[^szacunek]

**65% is a forecast.** NIPiP stated in April 2022 that in 2030, 65% of the nurses employed at that time would be of pensionable age, including those who had already reached it. It assumed an age of 60 for everyone, men included. This corresponds roughly to all employed nurses who were at least 51 in April 2022 (65.4%).[^nipip2022]

**The 2023 data give a lower figure.** By the end of 2030, registered nurses who were at least 52 in January 2023 will turn 60. With equal cohorts that is about {RETIREMENT_2030}% of the register, not 65.5% — the share of all groups from the age of 51.[^szacunek]

Retirement age does not mean leaving work. In 2022, 76,388 nurses and midwives who were already entitled to a pension were working — and each of them could retire at any moment.[^nipip2022]

The register does not say directly who the others are. According to it, just under 1% of registered nurses are abroad, and 8.7% are over 70, so some of them are presumably retired.[^nipip2023] The NIPiP website also does not explain which forms of work it counts as employment, so it is not known whether it covers all nurses working under contracts.[^crpip]

Compared with Europe, the workforce is thin. In 2023 there were 5.9 nurses working in Poland per 1,000 population, against an EU average of 8.5[^soh] and an OECD average of 9.2.[^hag] The profile of Poland in the State of Health in the EU report identifies the low number of doctors and nurses as a key constraint on the capacity of the system.[^soh]

## Inflow into the profession: forecasts versus data

The shortage forecasts that NIPiP published in 2021–2023 calculated the loss of staff as the difference between the people reaching retirement age and the people obtaining a licence to practise as a nurse or midwife (PWZ). They froze the inflow at a constant level. The 2022 report assumed 6,205 people a year — the 2019–2021 average for nurses and midwives combined — and assumed that all of them would take up work.[^model2022]

Meanwhile the inflow grew. In 2025, 10,152 people obtained a licence (9,153 nurses and 999 midwives), against 5,969 in 2019 and 4,202 in 2014.[^pwz] The 2023 NIPiP report itself noted that the forecast did not take into account the number of new licences, which had been rising since 2014.[^prognoza2023]

The employment data point in the same direction. This is good news, but not a solution. The inflow barely balances those reaching retirement age, the average age of the workforce is still rising, and the number of nurses per 1,000 population remains far from the EU average.

### Why the shortage figures differ

Three figures appear in the media. All of them come from the same calculation with a frozen inflow, but from different years and periods:

- **over 26,000** — the sum of the annual differences in 2023–2030 (exactly 26,273), with an inflow of 6,205 people a year; employed nurses and midwives; this figure was also announced in a NIPiP press release of 9.02.2024;[^prognoza2023]
- **28,889** — the same forecast counted from 2022, i.e. for 2022–2030;[^model2022]
- **36,293** — the fall in the number of employed nurses and midwives by 2030 in the older 2021 report, which froze the inflow at the 2020 level (5,693 licences).[^raport2021]

None of them takes into account the growth of the inflow in 2022–2025. They describe a scenario in which admissions would have stopped at the level of a few years earlier — and that did not happen.

## How many graduates stay in the profession

Training has clearly accelerated. In February 2023, 126 universities with accreditation from the minister of health could run nursing programmes.[^uczelnie] The number of new licences for nurses alone rose from 3,443 in 2014 to 9,153 in 2025.[^pwz]

Not every diploma turns into work in the profession, however. The measurement we were able to find concerns the 2020 cohort: 4,525 people obtained a nursing degree that year, and 2,504 of them, or about 55%, applied for a licence to practise by the end of 2021.[^demagog] This is the number of licence applications, not employment contracts, and without a breakdown into public and private facilities. Of the remaining 45%, all that is known is that they did not apply during that time. We did not find a newer measurement, and the NIPiP forecasts assumed that everyone with a new licence takes up work.[^model2022]

## Emigration: what is known and what is not

How many nurses leave is not known. The Supreme Audit Office (NIK) found in 2016 that Poland does not monitor the emigration of medical staff.[^nik] There are three indirect measures, and each measures something different.

**Certificates.** Anyone who wants to work in another EU country needs a certificate for the recognition of qualifications. The most were issued in 2015 (1,527). Since 2023 NIPiP has counted them with a changed scope (230 in 2025), so the newer years cannot be compared directly with the older ones.[^zaswiadczenia] A certificate points to an intention to leave rather than to departure itself.[^nik]

**Survey.** In a study that MedMedia Consulting (MMC) carried out for NIPiP in January 2023,[^mmc-data] just under 6% of working nurses were considering leaving within a year, and 1.7% definitely. Asked about the destination, they most often named the Scandinavian countries (28.6%), Germany (25.2%), the United Kingdom (16.5%) and Switzerland (12.7%).[^ankieta] These are declarations, not departures.

**OECD data.** According to the OECD report Health at a Glance 2025, about 64,000 Polish-born nurses were working in OECD countries in 2020/21. Among the countries of birth of migrant nurses, Poland ranks third, after the Philippines and India.[^oecd-migracja] This is, however, a stock, not a number of departures: it includes people who left many years ago, including those who obtained their qualification abroad.

## Society is ageing too

The workforce is ageing at the same time as the patients. In 2024, more than 7.7 million people aged 65 and over lived in Poland (20.6% of the population), and more than 1.6 million people aged 80+.[^gus2024] According to the GUS projection, the number of people aged 80+ will rise to 2.1 million by 2030 and to 3.5 million by 2060. People aged 65+ will then make up about 30% of the population.[^gusprognoza]

According to BGK, the state development bank, providing places for 6% of people aged 75+ in 2040 would require 276,000 places — almost twice as many as today.[^bgk] The growing number of the oldest patients increases the need for care, including nursing care, precisely in the years in which the largest cohorts of nurses reach retirement age.

## A public pay scale, undisclosed contracts

This site follows where public money in healthcare goes. With nurses, part of it is unusually visible. The minimum base pay of an employee in a healthcare provider follows from an act: it is the coefficient of the occupational group times a base amount announced every year. We set out the amounts for nurses, midwives and other professions in [Other medical professions: pay scale and numbers](/en/data/other-health-professions-pay/).

With specialist doctors it is the other way round: most of them work under contracts whose amounts nobody publishes (see [Contract or employment](/en/mechanisms/contract-vs-employment/)). Nurses working under contracts also fall outside this picture — their rates are known only from job advertisements.

A public minimum is also a point of reference in pay disputes. At the Provincial Integrated Hospital in Kalisz, arrears owed to staff, mainly nurses, reached about <Money value={37000000} scale="million" decimals={0} />. According to the new director, they resulted from incorrect grading and understated base rates.[^kalisz] We describe the finances of this hospital, including the cost of doctors’ contracts, in the overview of [hospital debt](/en/data/hospital-debt/).

Pay transparency will not remove the shortage of nurses: its source is above all the age structure of the workforce and an inflow that was too small for years. But how much money is left for staff pay is decided by the division of a single budget, and that division is only partly visible. The full picture would come from open data on the total paid from public money to each person, under a permanent identifier and without names — for doctors, nurses and other professions. That is what [our proposal](/en/demand/) is about.
