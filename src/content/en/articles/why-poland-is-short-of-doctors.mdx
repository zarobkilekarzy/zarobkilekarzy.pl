There are more places for future doctors at every stage. The admission limit for medicine rose from 8,170 places in 2019/20 to 10,730 in 2026/27,[^limity] the programme is run by 37 universities instead of 15 a decade ago,[^mz-app] and in the autumn 2025 specialist training session there were 14,598 places to fill.[^cmkp25] Even so, patients wait 4.2 months on average for a service in the public system.[^whc]

The Ministry of Health forecasts a nationwide surplus of doctors,[^mz-prognoza] and an analysis published by the medical self-government warned against training surplus doctors.[^nil] This need not contradict the waiting lists, because a place at medical school is not yet a doctor with a patient — in a field with a shortage, in a public hospital, in a county. This overview describes six reasons for that gap and refers to the analyses in which each of them is set out in detail.

## From decision to specialist: 12–13 years

A doctor admitted to medical school today will become a specialist only after more than a decade. The studies last six years, the postgraduate internship 13 months,[^staz] and specialist training usually four to six years, depending on the field.[^programy] With five- or six-year specialist training, 12–13 years pass from admission to medical school to the title of specialist.

The Ministry of Health does not say how many doctors complete specialist training each year, because many of them extend their training. As an approximation it gives the number of people who passed the State Specialisation Examination (PES): in 2016–2021 it was from **3,376** to **4,388** a year, including dentists.[^mz-pes] There are no newer figures in the public data of the Medical Examinations Centre (CEM), which publishes only the results of the written test, not of the whole examination.

Doctors who complete specialist training at this pace in 2026 began their studies in 2013–2014, when the admission limit was 5,352 and 5,510 places — roughly half the current number.[^limity] The cohorts of 2022–2025, admitted under limits of 9,500 to 10,500 places, will become specialists roughly in 2034–2038. The limit is, moreover, the upper bound on admissions set in a regulation, not the number of students admitted.

How a change in admissions translates into the number of doctors over the following decades is shown by the [doctor supply simulator](/analizy/symulator-podazy-lekarzy/) (in Polish). Where the low starting point came from is described in our analysis of [cutting medical school places](/en/analysis/cutting-medical-school-places/).

## Not every place at medical school gives Poland a doctor

Of the Poles who start medicine, about 80% obtain the diploma within the nominal time: in 2023/24 there were 4,776 graduates of Polish nationality, and 5,954 first-semester students in 2018/19. Most dropouts happen in the first year. Six years after graduation, 5% of graduates have no Social Insurance Institution (ZUS) contributions in Poland; this is an upper bound on emigration, because it also includes career breaks.[^mz-app] We describe the details in the analysis [Who drops out of medicine and who leaves](/en/analysis/medical-school-dropouts-and-emigration/).

Programmes taught in a foreign language are a separate pool: in 2026/27 they account for 1,838 of the 10,730 places in the limit.[^limity] They are attended mainly by foreigners, and of foreign graduates in medical and health sciences, fewer than 1% work in Poland a year after graduation (2021 diplomas).[^ela] More in the analysis [English Division](/en/analysis/english-division-medical-studies/).

Both losses become visible when admission limits are set against the number of doctors a few years later. In mid-2026, 20,457 doctors aged 26–30 held a licence to practise (PWZ) — roughly those admitted to medical school in 2015–2019, when the limits totalled about 37,100 places. So about 55% of the places turned into a licence to practise, but most of the difference consists of places in foreign-language programmes and for foreigners, not dropouts.[^nil-wiek]

The final examination is not a sieve in this calculation: 95–97% of graduates of Polish universities pass the Medical Final Examination (LEK) at the first attempt.[^cem] The path of doctors with diplomas from outside the European Union looks different — we describe it in the analysis [Doctors from outside the EU](/en/analysis/doctors-from-outside-the-eu/). Questions in the LEK format can be practised in the [exam simulator](/egzamin/) (in Polish).

## Specialist training places: more empty than filled

After the internship a doctor chooses a specialisation, and there are two kinds of places. Residency places are allocated in individual fields by the minister of health, who funds the resident doctor’s salary. Non-residency places are — in the wording of the act — the remaining vacant specialist training places in accredited units; in that case the facility pays for the doctor’s work.[^art16c][^prawo2022]

Applications for both kinds of place are submitted in the same qualification procedure. When there are more candidates than places, the order is set by a ranking based on the result of the Medical Final Examination.[^art16c] The facility therefore does not draw up the list of candidates, but for a non-residency place it must have the money for the salary — and in 2022 a representative of hospitals said plainly that they did not have the money for it.[^prawo2022]

There are more places than candidates. In the autumn 2025 session there were 14,598 places to fill, 7,995 valid applications were submitted, and 5,483 doctors confirmed acceptance of a place. 9,115 places, or 62%, remained empty.[^cmkp25] In the last four autumn sessions this share ranged from 56 to 74%, roughly two thirds in total.[^jesien]

<EmptyPlaces />

The empty places are above all non-residency places: in successive autumn sessions 82 to 91% of them were not filled, compared with 27 to 38% of residency places.[^jesien] The spring sessions look similar: in spring 2026, 52% of residency places and 89% of non-residency places remained empty.[^cmkp26] The chart covers only the autumn — together with the spring sessions, 7,200–8,200 doctors and dentists a year confirm acceptance of a specialist training place (2023–2025).[^rocznie]

Empty places do not mean that there are too few candidates in every field. In autumn 2022, 1,459 doctors did not qualify anywhere, although almost 12,000 places remained empty.[^rz2022] Candidates aim at fields where places are few: in autumn 2025 there were 7.47 candidates per place in plastic surgery, 6.94 in dermatology and 5.88 in endocrinology.[^konkurencja] In these three fields there were 17, 50 and 51 places nationwide, of which 7, 35 and 25 were residency places.[^wykaz] There were fewer candidates than places in, among others, internal medicine, emergency medicine and geriatrics.[^konkurencja] Internal medicine alone had 3,084 places, of which 2,329 were non-residency places.[^wykaz] We show the full list of fields — places, competition and pay rates — [by specialty](/en/data/residents-pay-by-specialty/).

An overall surplus of places does not mean either that there are enough of them everywhere. The Supreme Audit Office (NIK) found that in 2019–2022 the minister of health granted fewer residencies than the voivodes requested: in the spring sessions from 37.7% of the reported need in 2019 to 42.9% in 2022, in the autumn sessions from 71.4 to 58.7%. In anaesthesiology in ten voivodeships, and in paediatrics in eight, all the advertised residency places were allocated — in NIK’s view, more candidates could have been admitted had there been more places.[^nik-rez] How limits and modes of training affect access to the profession is described in the mechanism [Restricted access to the profession](/en/mechanisms/restricted-access-to-the-profession/).

## How many doctors there are depends on the definition

The figure most often cited in the dispute over the shortage is 3.9 doctors per 1,000 population. These are OECD data for 2023 on practising doctors: the OECD average is the same, and the European Union average is 4.3.[^oecd][^soh]

Polish sources count doctors in several ways, and each gives a different number:

According to Statistics Poland (GUS), in 2024 166,515 doctors living in Poland held a licence to practise, and 141,193 worked directly with patients — 25,322 fewer. Per 1,000 population that is 4.4 and 3.8 doctors respectively.[^gus2024] The register of the Supreme Medical Chamber (NIL) and the GUS data differ in definition and date, which is why we do not combine them in one calculation. The supply simulator starts from the chamber’s register and shows the number of doctors working with patients separately.[^crl]

The workforce is also older than the number alone suggests: of the doctors registered as practising, 24,595, or 15%, are aged 71 or over.[^crl] In the simulator this group declines only at the rate of deaths, because that is how the register behaves, and successive cohorts work by default until the age of 70.

Comparisons over time require care because of a change in method. The figure of 2.4 doctors per 1,000 population keeps coming back in the debate — a GUS indicator from 2010, from the time when doctors were counted from facility reports (the MZ-88 survey), which according to the ministry covered about 70% of healthcare providers and about 13% of medical practices. Today GUS counts doctors from administrative registers — among others those of the medical chamber, ZUS and the National Health Fund (NFZ) — so results from before and after the change should not be read as a single series.[^gus-metoda]

Mixing definitions causes a similar problem. When in July 2024 the minister of health set 4.2 doctors per 1,000 population — that is, everyone with a licence to practise according to the chamber — against the EU average, Demagog, a Polish fact-checking organisation, rated the statement as manipulation: for Poland the OECD counts doctors working directly with patients, and by that measure Poland had 3.4 doctors per 1,000 population in 2021.[^demagog]

## Doctors are lacking in specific places and fields

The national average hides a wide spread. In 2024 there were from 2.6 doctors working with patients per 1,000 population in Lubuskie voivodeship to 4.7 in Mazowieckie.[^gus2024] The OECD and European Commission report on Poland points to shortages in specific specialties and regions, especially in small counties around large cities and in rural areas, and to a very low share of family doctors: 6.7% of all doctors, against an EU average of 19.9%.[^soh] The ministry, too, while forecasting a nationwide surplus of doctors, notes that shortages may occur in some regions and fields.[^mz-prognoza]

An example from the local press: in June 2024 the county hospital in Radomsko was looking for a head of its paediatric ward, closed during the COVID-19 epidemic. According to Gazeta Radomszczańska, the head of the ward could earn as much as <Money value={50000} /> a month, more than the hospital director, and there were no candidates.[^radomsko] This is a single case, not a measurement, but it shows what a shortage in one field and one county looks like.

## Contracts: the same doctors in several places

Most specialists are not salaried. According to an AOTMiT working document from February 2026, 73% of specialist doctors work under contracts.[^aotmit] Working-time and rest standards are not calculated per person across all contracts: since 2015 NIK has been calling for such a change in the law, and in a 2023 report it found that the recommendation had not been implemented.[^nik-czas] The same doctor can therefore fill rotas in several facilities, and gaps in one place are patched with the hours of the same people. We describe this in the mechanisms [Contract or employment](/en/mechanisms/contract-vs-employment/) and [Multiple jobs](/en/mechanisms/multiple-jobs-parallel-contracts/).

Hospital directors explain high rates by the lack of candidates: whoever cannot fill an on-call shift has to outbid the neighbouring facility.[^rp-stawki] Not everyone agrees. The director of the National Institute of Public Health PZH, formerly a vice-president of the NFZ, points out that rates were also pushed up by doctors moving to civil-law contracts after the change in working-time rules, negotiations by whole groups of doctors, contracts with pay calculated as a percentage of revenue from the NFZ, and the pandemic.[^rp-stawki]

How often very high payments occur depends on what is counted. According to AOTMiT, the median monthly pay under a contract is <Money value={25595} /> gross, and 625 contracts, or 1.2%, were for more than <Money value={100000} /> a month.[^aotmit] The agency, however, counts contracts in each facility separately, not people. Its president estimates that after adding up payments from several facilities 5–10% of doctors could exceed this threshold, which Rynek Zdrowia converted into 7,000–14,000 people; this is an estimate, not a measurement.[^rz-milion] We show the difference between a contract and a person in [The distribution of doctors’ pay](/en/data/doctor-pay-distribution/), and collect the highest disclosed amounts in [record payments](/en/data/record-doctor-payments/).

## Too few or too many doctors?

The Ministry of Health forecasts that by 2034 the supply of doctors will exceed demand, with a growing surplus. It assumes that a doctor works 161 hours a month on average, and does not give the method or the data of the forecast.[^mz-prognoza] An analysis published by the Supreme Medical Chamber in June 2024 stated that “we are heading towards a significant surplus of doctors” (our translation); the chamber notes that this is not the official position of its council.[^nil]

On the other side are data on access to treatment: an average wait of 4.2 months for a service,[^whc] shortages in specific fields and regions identified by the OECD and the European Commission,[^soh] and the findings of NIK from its audit of 2018–2022, according to which the minister of health prepared neither a model for estimating the need for medical staff nor a long-term strategy for its development.[^nik-model]

This much can be established from public data: per head of population, Poland has as many practising doctors as the OECD countries on average, and fewer than the EU countries on average. They are unevenly distributed between regions and fields, and the structure of specialist training places does not match candidates’ choices. What cannot be calculated is how much work and how much public money falls to one person across all contracts together. The second of these figures would be shown by the open pseudonymised data we call for in [the site’s proposal](/en/demand/).
