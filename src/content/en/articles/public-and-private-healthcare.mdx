In the morning, the same orthopaedic surgeon operates at a county hospital; in the afternoon, they see patients at a private practice two streets away. Under the National Health Fund (NFZ), the wait for a hip replacement is almost a year and a half on average.[^whc] For a paid visit to the practice, it is as long as the doctor’s schedule dictates. The patient sees two queues and two price lists. The doctor has one day and one schedule, which they divide between the public and the private purse. This text describes how the two systems share money, risk and doctors — in the United States, which is seen as the model of a market, and in Poland, where both sectors operate side by side, often under one roof.

## Who pays and who treats

“Public” and “private” healthcare are shorthand terms that combine two different things: who pays for treatment and who owns the facility. A county hospital is public in both senses. A private company with an NFZ contract for MRI scans or dialysis is private by ownership but public by money — the patient pays nothing, the Fund pays. Conversely, a public hospital can sell commercial services.

<Comparison table={MATRIX} />

So when we speak of “socialising costs and privatising profits”, we do not mean that companies make money and SPZOZs lose it. The point is which costs — training, on-call duty, difficult cases, losses — remain with the public payer, and which revenues can be moved to where the patient, the service and the price can be chosen. ## The American model: a market paid for by the state

In 2024 the United States spent US$5.3 trillion on health, or 18.0% of GDP — <Money value={15474} currency="USD" /> per resident.[^cms] Most working-age Americans are insured through their employer. People over 65 are insured by the federal Medicare programme, the poorest by the state-run Medicaid. Doctors and hospitals are paid for each service, and each insurer negotiates prices separately. In 2024 private insurance covered 31% of spending, Medicare 21%, Medicaid 18% and patients out of pocket 11%.[^cms]

This is not a purely private system. According to the OECD, public money financed 54% of American health spending in 2023.[^oecd-finanse] Per resident, public programmes alone — Medicare, Medicaid and other government programmes — cost {formatEN(USA_PUBLIC)} US dollars at purchasing power parity in 2024. That is more than all health spending, public and private combined, in 27 of the 37 other OECD countries, including the United Kingdom, France and Poland.[^oecd-sha]

For all this money Americans do not live longer. Life expectancy in 2023 was the same in the US and in Poland — 78.4 years — with spending per resident 3.5 times higher.[^oecd-profil] Roughly one in twelve US residents, 27.2 million people, had no insurance at all in 2024.[^nhis] Medical debts that appeared in credit reports reached <Money value={88e9} currency="USD" scale="billion" decimals={0} />.[^cfpb]

What makes the system expensive is above all prices. In 2022 private insurers paid hospitals on average 254% of the rates that Medicare pays for the same services, and for doctors’ services — 184%.[^rand] A doctor in the US earns on average <Money value={277260} currency="USD" /> a year, almost four times as much as the average worker.[^bls] And there are fewer practising doctors than in Poland: 2.7 per 1,000 residents compared with 3.9.[^oecd-sha]

This model shows clearly how costs go to the common purse and profits to those who are able to choose:

The data for this list come from federal agencies and Congress.[^cms][^medpac][^aha][^crs][^kff] It also includes a mechanism that Americans considered dangerous enough to ban. A doctor may not refer Medicare patients to a facility in which they have a financial interest — unless one of the narrow exceptions applies. The audit office of Congress calculated that doctors who referred patients for tests to their own units ordered about twice as many MRI and CT scans as other doctors.[^gao] We found no similar ban in the Polish acts on healthcare services and on medical activity. The Code of Medical Ethics only forbids recommending a healthcare provider for the sake of one’s own benefit.[^kel]

## Poland: a public core, a private overlay

Poland, like Germany, Czechia and Austria, has an insurance-based system: contributions go to a single payer, which buys services from public and private facilities. Alongside it there is a market in which the patient pays directly or through a subscription or an insurance policy from the employer. Both markets are served by the same people. In a 2026 survey by the medical self-government, about 55% of doctors said they worked in both the public and the private part of the system, and 11.9% only in the private part.[^nil] The sample was not random, but the scale is clear: for most specialists, the private practice and the hospital with an NFZ contract are two workplaces in a single day.

### A second purse: practices, subscriptions and patients’ own pockets

Part of the private market consists of fully commercial facilities with no NFZ contract at all: specialists’ practices and nationwide chains of clinics that sell subscriptions, most often to employers as an addition to pay. The largest of the listed chains had €1.24 billion of revenue in Poland in 2025.[^medicover] According to CBOS, a Polish public opinion research centre, 36% of adults have a medical subscription or a health insurance policy, including 17% who receive it from their employer. 11% of adults are treated exclusively outside the NFZ, and 53% use both systems.[^cbos]

This market is not a separate world. It largely employs the same specialists who see patients under the NFZ, and patients go to a private practice mostly to skip the queue — that is how 79% of those who pay explain it.[^cbos2023] The closest example to Poland’s is Austria. There a doctor can have a contract with a health insurance fund — then the patient pays nothing — or work as an “elective doctor” without a contract, for a full fee, part of which the fund reimburses to the patient. The number of fund doctors has stood still, while the number of elective doctors has grown by 61% since 2004.[^soh-at] Austria also has more doctors per resident than Poland — so the private part is growing not only because of a shortage of doctors, but also because working outside the fund is often more advantageous for them.

## The same pathology, a different scale

The Polish system does not resemble the American one: insurance is universal, and prices for NFZ patients are zero. In both countries, however, the same mechanism operates — costs that cannot be chosen stay with the public payer, while revenues that can be chosen may move to the private side.

## More money, fewer visits, a longer queue

Our thesis is this: the weaker public care is, the stronger private care becomes. Weakness here does not mean, however, that public money is not growing — it is growing fast. It means that it buys less and less treatment. When the public system cannot offer an appointment, patients who can afford it pay privately, and private practice becomes more lucrative for doctors. The longer the public queue, the stronger the second price list — and the more the hospital has to pay for the same hours.

Since 2015 NFZ costs have grown {formatEN(Q.budget, 1)} times. The average waiting time for a service, measured by the Watch Health Care Foundation, increased by {formatEN(Q.queue)}% over the same period and in autumn 2025 reached 4.2 months, the highest in the history of the survey.[^nfz-plany][^whc] Those who can, go to a private practice: in 2025, 40% of adults had seen a specialist outside the NFZ within six months, compared with 29% in 2018.[^cbos] We show how the NFZ budget, the queue and the price of a private visit changed year by year in the analysis [Private visits: the longer the waiting list, the higher the price](/en/analysis/private-visits-queues-and-prices/).

For this money the NFZ buys fewer and fewer visits per zloty, and in primary care (POZ) — fewer visits overall. In the first half of 2026 the Fund spent 14.8% more on primary care than a year earlier, while family doctors provided 11.5% fewer consultations — 7.6 million fewer. The number of patients fell by only 1.8%.[^nfz-q2] In specialist outpatient clinics the number of visits is rising, but more slowly than the money: in 2022–2024 costs rose by 55.6%, and the number of services by 13.7%.[^nfz2024]

<Comparison table={POZ_TABLE} />

The NFZ report does not give the reasons for the fall in primary care consultations. Family doctors list possible causes: a difference in the infection season (data for the whole year will give the full picture), annual prescriptions, which free chronically ill patients from visits for a prescription, and the consolidation of facilities, after which some of them shorten their opening hours.[^rz-poz] One thing is certain: a primary care doctor is not paid per visit but receives a fixed rate for each patient on the list, so more money need not mean more consultations. We describe how this rate works in the analysis [Money per patient in primary care](/en/analysis/primary-care-capitation/).

Despite the growing budget, Poland spends less on health than the average EU country: 7.84% of GDP compared with an EU average of 10.16%. Public spending per resident in purchasing power standards was {formatEN(PL_PUBLIC_PPS)} in 2024 compared with an EU average of {formatEN(EU_PUBLIC_PPS)}, or {publicVsEu}% of it.[^eurostat] Private money also plays a bigger role than elsewhere: patients covered 16.6% of spending out of pocket (EU: 15.1%), and subscriptions, insurance policies and other private spending 6.5% (EU: 4.6%).[^eurostat]

The main barrier is time, not price. In the 2025 EU-SILC survey, 2.2% of Polish adults said they had not had a medical examination or treatment because the waiting list was too long. In the EU such people made up 1.2%. Cost was the reason for 0.5% in Poland and 1.0% in the EU.[^silc]

The share of private money in total spending fell, from {formatEN(share(2015), 1)}% in 2015 to {formatEN(share(2024), 1)}% in 2024, because public money grew faster: nominally {formatEN(NRZ[2024].pub / NRZ[2015].pub, 2)} times compared with {formatEN(NRZ[2024].priv / NRZ[2015].priv, 2)} times.[^nrz2015][^nrz2024] The private market did not shrink in zloty terms, however — it almost doubled — and the queue grew longer. If the additional public money bought more doctors’ hours, the queue should be getting shorter and private practices should be losing clients. The opposite happened.

Comparative research describes this relationship more cautiously. A 2004 analysis of OECD countries with parallel private financing concludes that a shift towards private money in the balance does more harm than good to public systems, although the effects depend on its form.[^tuohy] In Australia, a long expected queue led people to buy private insurance, and in public hospitals private patients were given higher priority.[^johar] A 2011 review of research on doctors working in two sectors, however, finds little hard evidence of their impact on public queues.[^socha]

## The private practice as a door to the hospital

The wait for a specialist visit under the NFZ is months, for planned surgery often more than a year, and in extreme cases years. A private visit shortens the first queue, but it can also be a door to the second.

The mechanism is simple. Any doctor can refer a patient to hospital, including a doctor in a private practice — only a specialist outpatient clinic requires a referral from a doctor with an NFZ contract. The medical category and the date are set by the hospital, often the one where the doctor from the practice works.[^art57][^art20] A paid consultation is therefore not just a faster visit. It can open the way to the ward where the same doctor works, and to a procedure paid for by the NFZ. If as a result the patient gets a date earlier than their condition justifies, the paid visit buys a place in the public queue.

Doctors themselves point to this problem. In the medical self-government survey, 41.4% of respondents named adding patients outside a transparent order as a problem of combining the sectors, and 27.3% — moving patients from the public queue to private visits.[^nil] NFZ audits in 2026 found admissions outside the queue order on a large scale: at a clinic of a Warsaw university hospital, 70% of the audited admissions did not follow the queue order, and at a clinic of one of the Warsaw hospitals run by a government ministry — more than 97%.[^uck][^mswia] In neither audit did the NFZ confirm that patients reached the ward through a private practice. In the first, such an allegation was made in an anonymous letter from staff; in the second, donations to a foundation linked to the hospital were pointed to, which the ministry denies. How often a private practice opens the way to a ward is not known, because nobody matches paid visits against admissions financed by the NFZ. We describe this in detail in the section [A ticket through the private practice](/en/analysis/private-visits-queues-and-prices/#a-ticket-through-the-private-practice).

## Competition for doctors

A hospital does not buy a “doctor” but their hours, and specialists’ hours cannot be bought in quickly — 12–13 years pass from admission to medical school to the title of specialist. The same limited pool of hours is sought by private practices, clinics with NFZ contracts and several hospitals in the area. It looks like a tug of war in which the rope has two ends, but many are pulling.

Each step has its source.[^nik-zlecenia][^lop][^nowogard][^pinczow][^elk][^opole][^oddzialy][^kotwica] In other hospitals it looks similar. When the county hospital in Iłża was looking for doctors for its internal medicine ward in February 2026, candidates demanded PLN 350–500 an hour, and the director called these amounts “lethal” for the hospital (our translation).[^ilza] The hospital in Bartoszyce, with <Money value={90.7e6} scale="million" /> of debt, paid an orthopaedic surgeon about <Money value={113000} /> a month in 2026, and yet since August it has been merging three wards because it lacks doctors.[^bartoszyce] Nationwide, “staff shortages” were the reason for 321 of the 539 decisions to suspend or close wards; most often they concerned obstetrics. This is an editorial compilation from the justifications that hospitals sent to the Ministry of Health, and “staff shortages” can mean both a lack of candidates and doctors who left because they received a better offer.[^oddzialy] Almost 91% of county hospitals ended 2025 with a loss on their core activity.[^zpp]

The arithmetic is simple. With a fixed budget, the number of hours bought is inversely proportional to the rate: if an hour becomes 50% more expensive, the hospital buys a third fewer hours for the same money. Higher rates do not bring in new doctors, because their number is set by decisions taken years ago. What they do is make the same doctors move their hours to where they are paid more. The hospital that lost the bidding suspends the ward; patients travel further or go private. The weaker hospital has less money for the next round. That is how the spiral closes — and how additional public money turns into a more expensive hour instead of a shorter queue.

Other countries know this spiral too. In 2014/15 English hospitals spent <Money value={3.3e9} currency="GBP" scale="billion" /> on agency medical staff, half as much again as five years earlier, and the UK’s National Audit Office found a statistically significant link between this spending and hospital deficits.[^nao] In Sweden all regions limited the hiring of agency staff, and in 2024 spending on hired doctors fell by 23%, and on hired staff overall by 35.8%.[^skr] The bidding can be stopped when buyers stop outbidding one another — but for that, one needs to know who pays how much.

## Socialising costs, privatising profits

In Poland, as in the US, part of the costs of healthcare stays on the public side regardless of who provides the service and who profits from it. We describe this mechanism with five examples. None of them means that anyone is breaking the law, and some are simply tasks that rest with the state. Together, however, they show where the costs lie and where the margin is.

<NumberedList items={COST_SHIFTS} />

Each point has its source and its limits.[^druk1569][^nik-budzet][^kredyt][^gus-60][^cbos][^nik-kardio][^nik-poludniowy][^zpp-samorzady][^medicover] NIK’s findings on cardiology concern 2011–2014, and pricing has changed since then. The private group’s result does not prove that it makes money at the expense of public hospitals — it is a different business model, in which the patient and the scope of services can be chosen. Local government does not have to cover a hospital’s losses — it only may, although in practice it does so regularly.[^uodl] There is one common denominator: costs that cannot be chosen — training, on-call duty, the patient with multiple conditions, the deficit — stay on the public side. Revenues that can be chosen may move to the private side.

There are also costs that nobody counts. Complications after procedures performed commercially, in Poland or abroad, are treated in emergencies at the NFZ’s expense, but there is no register that would make it possible to establish how much this costs.

<Aside title="The doctor is not the culprit here" variant="warn" label="How to read this mechanism">
A doctor who accepts a better offer behaves rationally — just like an IT specialist or an engineer. According to AOTMiT, the median monthly pay of doctors, salaried and under contract, is PLN 24,000–27,000; about 1.5% of them earn more than <Money value={100000} /> a month.[^aotmit] The problem does not lie in the pay of the average doctor, but in the fact that several hundred hospitals bid for the same hours blindly, and the public payer cannot see what it is buying.
</Aside>

## Ban the combination?

The simplest answer to the conflict of interest is to forbid doctors to work in both sectors. The idea comes back regularly in politics. In 2026 it was announced both by PiS (Law and Justice), which wants to write the ban into the party’s programme, and by the New Left (Nowa Lewica) in a draft on healthcare financing. The justification sounds similar to this text: private entities make money, while difficult cases remain in the public system.[^prawo-zakaz] In an IBRiS poll for the daily Rzeczpospolita in July 2026, 53.5% of respondents said that doctors should not combine work in both sectors, and 40.7% that they may.[^ibris]

Doctors see it differently. In the medical self-government survey, a full ban was supported by 15.1% of respondents, and the most support — 71.9% — went to separating public and private queues, schedules and billing.[^nil] The National Trade Union of Doctors (OZZL) warns that a ban would push some doctors into the private sector.[^prawo-zakaz] The Presidium of the Supreme Medical Council considers that rigid restrictions amid staff shortages would reduce the number of NFZ services and increase the number of purely private ones.[^nrl] The act on pay limits for medical staff, which the Sejm passed on 22 October 2026 and which is now before the Senate, does not introduce a ban.[^glosowanie] It limits total working time and pay and gives the NFZ insight into the commercial services of facilities that have contracts with it.[^druk3157]

A ban solves one problem — the conflict of interest — but may deepen another. With a fixed number of doctors, some specialists would choose private practice, the public queue would grow longer, and rates for those who stayed would rise — the same bidding mechanism, only with a stronger opponent. Ireland is taking a gradual route: since 2023 new consultants in the public health service receive only “public only” contracts. In August 2025, 64% of consultants had them, but an internal audit found that there is no system to check the phasing out of private practice in public hospitals.[^irl] We describe the British and Irish solutions in more detail in the section [Ban the combination?](/en/analysis/private-visits-queues-and-prices/#ban-the-combination) of the analysis of private visits. The common conclusion: without data on who works where and how much, it is impossible either to enforce a ban or to check whether one is needed.

## What the private sector contributes

The private part of the system is not only a cost. In 2024 it contributed <Money value={66.4e9} scale="billion" />, which the public budget would not have put up.[^nrz2024] Private companies with NFZ contracts increase capacity where the payer buys volume: after the limits on cataract operations were lifted, the average waiting time fell from 398 days in 2019 to 55 days at the end of 2024.[^zacma] Working in two sectors may also increase doctors’ total hours. In Norway, where more than 12,000 hospital doctors were studied, such dual practice was not associated with waiting times, and better-paid extra hours in hospital reduced the inclination to work privately.[^norwegia]

This last finding shows well where the line lies. The private sector helps when the public payer knows what it is buying and can pay enough for an hour that the doctor does not need to look for it elsewhere. It does harm when the public system is too weak to pay competitively and too blind to see where its hours and patients are flowing out.

## A boundary nobody can see

Today nobody in Poland knows how many hours one doctor works for the NFZ and how many privately, how much they receive in total from public money from all their workplaces, or how much a hospital in the neighbouring county pays for the same hour. The NFZ does not have this knowledge, nor do hospital directors or patients. The government bill will give the Fund insight into the commercial services of facilities, but the payer will see individual facilities, not the total for one person.[^druk3157]

Swedish regions limited the bidding for agency staff because every year they publicly count how much they spend on it.[^skr] Americans banned referrals to doctors’ own facilities because they calculated how much it costs, and they publish Medicare payments to doctors with names.[^gao][^cms-npi] In both cases the first step was a number. Our proposal is more modest than American practice: public amounts under a permanent pseudonym, without names. Without such data, every additional zloty for public healthcare may buy more treatment — or merely raise the price of an hour in the next round of bidding.

<Aside title="What these data do not show" variant="note">
Nobody measures the flow of doctors and hours between the public and the private sector. That is why the weakest link of the spiral described here is its beginning: it is not possible today to measure by how much a better offer from a private practice raises the rate in a hospital. Among the ward suspensions described here, none documents doctors moving to the private sector — what is documented is bidding between hospitals. Part of the rise in hospital pay costs results from the act on minimum pay in healthcare, not from the contract market; the available data do not allow the two to be separated. Queues also grow longer for reasons unrelated to money, including the ageing of patients. Comparisons with the EU and the OECD rely on health accounts whose methodology is sometimes revised.
</Aside>
