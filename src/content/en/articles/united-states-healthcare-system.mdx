The United States spends the largest share of national income on health among OECD countries and has no single payer: treatment is paid for by insurers chosen by employers, two large public programmes and patients themselves. We describe how this system works according to data from US federal agencies, and check what is public in it. More is known there about public money than in Poland — payments from the programme for older people are published for every doctor by first name and surname.

## Many payers instead of one

An American of working age is usually insured through an employer with a private insurer. People over 65 and some people with disabilities are covered by the federal Medicare programme, and people on low incomes by Medicaid, run jointly by the federal government and the states. Anyone who fits none of these groups can buy a policy on their own.

The US agency counts spending differently from the OECD (18.0% of GDP against 17.2%), so the two figures should not be read as contradictory. For the USA the OECD has no comparable data on unmet healthcare needs or on the share of spending covered from compulsory sources. 75% of Americans are satisfied with the availability of good care where they live, against an OECD average of 64%.[^oecd-noty]

## A public fee schedule and negotiated prices

Medicare pays doctors for every service according to a national fee schedule. Each service is assigned a point value, which is multiplied by a conversion factor set every year: in 2026 it is <Money value={33.40} currency="USD" />, and for doctors participating in alternative payment models <Money value={33.57} currency="USD" />.[^mpfs]

Private insurers negotiate prices with each hospital and each practice separately. According to a RAND analysis covering 2022 prices, they paid on average 254% of Medicare rates for hospital care including the associated doctors’ fees, and 184% for doctors’ services alone.[^rand]

## What the patient pays

Insurance through an employer cost on average <Money value={28303} currency="USD" /> a year for a family in 2026, of which the worker paid <Money value={7471} currency="USD" />. Before the policy starts to pay, the patient covers the costs alone up to a deductible, which averaged <Money value={1955} currency="USD" /> a year for single coverage — among workers whose policy has such a deductible.[^kff-ehbs]

## Doctors’ work and pay

According to the Bureau of Labor Statistics, salaried doctors earned on average <Money value={277260} currency="USD" /> a year in May 2025, with a median of <Money value={271090} currency="USD" />. That is 3.97 times the average pay of all workers. Family doctors earned on average <Money value={255820} currency="USD" /> and cardiologists <Money value={454940} currency="USD" />.[^bls] The statistics cover employees only — practice owners are not included. The OECD does not publish doctors’ pay for the USA that is comparable with other countries.

## Public and private work at the same time

In the USA there is no division into public and private doctors — the same doctor sees patients of Medicare, Medicaid and private insurers. A doctor can formally opt out of Medicare and bill patients directly; in November 2024, 12,244 doctors had done so, or 1.2%.[^kff-optout] In 2019, 89% of office-based doctors accepted new Medicare patients and 91% new patients with private insurance.[^kff-nowi]

Conflict of interest is regulated elsewhere. An act (the Stark Law) prohibits a doctor from referring Medicare patients for designated services — including laboratory tests and imaging — to an entity with which the doctor or a member of the doctor’s immediate family has a financial relationship, unless one of the statutory exceptions applies.[^stark] The federal audit office (GAO) found, on 2010 data, that doctors referring to their own diagnostics ordered about twice as many MRI and CT scans as other doctors, which cost Medicare about <Money value={109000000} currency="USD" scale="million" decimals={0} />.[^gao]

## What is public

**Medicare payments to every doctor.** Every year CMS publishes a dataset in which every doctor billing Medicare for individual services (Part B of the programme) appears by first name and surname, with the practice address, specialty, number of patients and services, and the amount paid by the programme. The latest data are for 2024. The agency first released such data on 9 April 2014 — for 2012, for more than 880,000 providers, who were paid <Money value={77000000000} currency="USD" scale="billion" decimals={0} />.[^cms-dane] [^cms-2014] The dataset shows payments for services in one programme — without private insurers and without the costs of running a practice — so it is not information about a doctor’s income.

**Payments from industry.** An act requires manufacturers of medicines and medical devices to report payments to a doctor and to a teaching hospital — apart from small ones, below the statutory threshold — and the agency publishes them by name (Open Payments). For 2025 this is 17.07 million records worth <Money value={14670000000} currency="USD" scale="billion" decimals={2} />, including for 667,898 doctors. Failure to report a payment carries a penalty of from <Money value={1000} currency="USD" /> to <Money value={10000} currency="USD" />, and a knowing failure from <Money value={10000} currency="USD" /> to <Money value={100000} currency="USD" />.[^open-payments] [^sunshine]

**Hospital prices.** Hospitals must publish their prices, including the rates negotiated with insurers. By 18 November 2026 CMS had announced 29 civil monetary penalty decisions against 28 hospitals that had not done so.[^hpt]

**Pay of federal employees.** The name, position and annual salary of federal employees — including doctors employed by the federal government — are public.[^cfr]

**What is not visible.** Private insurers’ payments to a doctor and the doctor’s total income are not public. The national register of compensation payments and sanctions against doctors (NPDB) is accessible only to authorised institutions; a doctor sees only their own entries in it. Only a dataset without identifying data, for research purposes, is publicly available.[^npdb]

## Waiting times

We found no national official measurement of the waiting time for an appointment or for surgery. Public statistics measure a different barrier to access: lack of insurance, which in 2025 affected 26.7 million people.[^census]

## Professional and criminal liability

The licence to practise is granted and withdrawn by state medical boards. Patients pursue compensation in the civil courts, and the insurer reports every payment to the national NPDB register — by the end of 2025 it had accumulated about 529,000 such reports, concerning doctors and other health professions.[^npdb]

Abuses in billing public programmes are a separate category. In fiscal year 2025 settlements and judgments under the False Claims Act exceeded <Money value={6800000000} currency="USD" scale="billion" />, of which more than <Money value={5700000000} currency="USD" scale="billion" /> concerned healthcare.[^fca] In June 2026 the Department of Justice announced charges against 455 people, including 90 doctors and other licensed medical professionals, in cases involving claims of more than <Money value={6500000000} currency="USD" scale="billion" />.[^takedown] These are charges, not proven facts, and the amount is the value of the claims called into question.

## Method

We base the description on data from US federal agencies: CMS (spending, the fee schedule, the datasets on payments and on payments from industry), the Census Bureau (insurance), the Bureau of Labor Statistics (pay) and the Department of Justice (abuses), and for insurance premiums — on the annual employer survey of the KFF foundation. The comparative indicators for the USA and Poland come from the OECD country notes, not from Eurostat.

We do not give the effects of the budget act of July 2025 on the number of insured people, or the statistics of the state medical boards — we were unable to open the source documents.
