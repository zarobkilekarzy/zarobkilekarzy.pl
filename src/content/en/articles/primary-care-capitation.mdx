Of all the money the National Health Fund (NFZ) passes on to doctors, the easiest to count is the money in primary health care (POZ): a public annual rate for every patient registered on the list. At the same time, it is hard to find a less transparent stream when one asks how much of it a specific doctor keeps. On top of that, the construction of the rate quietly affects whom it is better to examine on the spot and whom to refer onwards.

## Payment is per patient on the list, not per visit

A primary care doctor is not paid per visit. The patient submits a declaration of choice and joins the so-called active list. From that moment the provider receives 1/12 of the annual capitation rate for that patient every month — even if the patient does not come in once during the whole year. The rate is multiplied by an age coefficient: a small child or an older person “weighs” several times more than an adult in the prime of life.

The formula from the NFZ order: **amount due = number of patients on the list × 1/12 of the annual rate × age coefficient**. The rate is annual, not monthly — descriptions often get this wrong by a factor of twelve.

This is not an abuse but a deliberate choice: capitation is meant to reward care for the whole population and prevention, not the multiplication of visits. What matters more is how much this stream weighs[^sprawozdanie][^gus] and how it grows.

### How much the rate is and how it has grown

The base rate (coefficient 1.0) rose in nominal terms from <Money value={154.2} decimals={2} /> in 2018 to <Money value={232.56} /> from 1 July 2026, i.e. by 50.8% in eight years.[^stawki][^z65] In the first half of 2026 it was PLN 226.08. The largest jump came in {biggest.year}: {biggest.label} on the previous year. Since 2022 the rate has been raised every year from 1 July, in 2022 and 2023 together with the statutory pay rises in healthcare.

### Who “weighs” the most

The same rate is multiplied by a coefficient that depends on the patient’s age. For the practice, the youngest and the oldest are several times more “valuable” than an adult in the prime of life.

This is the practice’s revenue from capitation alone, not the doctor’s income. Before anything is left over, the practice has to pay from this amount for all the care of the list, including tests. The coefficient of 1.6 is our estimate of the average for a typical age structure — children and older people push it above 1.0. This is a model, not a measurement.

## What the practice pays for out of this amount

Capitation does not reach the doctor “net”. It is a budget from which the provider funds all the care of the list. One item distinguishes primary care from a specialist outpatient clinic and surprises many patients: here the cost of routine tests is borne by the doctor, out of capitation. In specialist care tests are contracted separately, whereas in primary care each test ordered reduces what is left.

To be precise: not everything comes out of capitation. Extended diagnostics — for example ferritin, vitamin B12 or anti-HCV antibodies — are paid for from a separate delegated budget (*budżet powierzony*), and tests such as Holter monitoring, spirometry or Doppler ultrasound from the coordinated care budget. Since 1 May 2026 a primary care doctor can order more than 100 tests from the delegated budget, Rynek Zdrowia reported.[^100-badan] The core, however — the routine tests at almost every visit — is still charged to capitation.

The list of tests financed out of the rate is set out in annex 1 to the regulation on guaranteed primary care services.[^rozporzadzenie] The division into tests funded from capitation and from separate budgets is explained by Medycyna Praktyczna[^mp] and the Silesian branch of the NFZ.[^nfz-slask]

## Cheaper to refer than to test

Since basic tests are paid for out of capitation, the construction has a side effect that nobody designed but that follows directly from it. It is reinforced by an NFZ rule on dividing costs between levels of care. When referring a patient to a specialist, primary care performs only the tests needed to confirm the initial diagnosis. The specialist outpatient clinic (AOS) that takes over the patient performs any further tests at its own cost — this is how the NFZ describes the rules.[^nfz-skierowania]

The practice’s calculation is simple: a test ordered on the spot is a cost out of capitation, while a referral shifts that cost onwards — the diagnostics are covered by the AOS, and the practice keeps the full rate for the patient. This is not an accusation of bad faith or a profit for the doctor, but a feature of a construction in which the cheaper path and the clinically best path can diverge.

There is a bitter paradox in this. Capitation was meant to reward prevention, but since every test is a cost, the same rate discourages the diagnostics that prevention requires.

What actually follows from this — with a note on what cannot be proved:

- **Weaker diagnostics on the spot — a documented risk, not proof of a widespread practice.** The broadest NIK audit[^nik] (2012–2014) showed that primary care does not make full use of its own capabilities: the cardiovascular disease prevention programme covered about 3% of those eligible each year, and a cervical smear result was recorded in fewer than 7% of women’s records. The gap only began to be seriously addressed with the delegated budget in 2022.
- **Waiting lists for specialists — real, but with many causes.** The average wait for a specialist consultation is 4.2 months, and for a diagnostic test 2.8 months;[^barometr] since 2012 waiting times have doubled. Excess referrals from primary care are one factor, alongside a shortage of specialists and growing demand.
- **A move to paid care — a broader phenomenon than one mechanism.** Long waiting lists push some patients to private surgeries. Private spending is already more than 20% of all health spending: <Money value={66400000000} scale="billion" decimals={1} /> in 2024, about 23% of current expenditure.[^wydatki] How much of this results from the construction of primary care cannot be calculated.

<Aside title="The patch reduces the incentive, but does not remove it">

The state has recognised this incentive. The delegated budget — since 2022, and since 1 May 2026 extended to more than 100 tests — pays for tests separately, so that ordering them does not burden capitation and patients are not sent to specialist clinics just for a test.[^mzdrowie] This budget is limited, however. Once it runs out, a referral can again be the cheaper path.

</Aside>

The public money that comes in for a list can be counted to the grosz. What the construction does with the money and with patients — how much diagnostics is pushed onwards, who ends up on a waiting list instead of being tested on the spot — nobody measures.

## A low entry, a high ceiling

Most of the best-paid fields require six years of study and five to six years of residency, for which one has to compete. The path into primary care is wider. Under Article 6 of the Act on primary health care,[^ustawa] services may be provided by:

- a specialist in family medicine or a doctor in training in that specialisation,
- a doctor with a second-degree specialisation in general medicine,
- a paediatrician after a course in family medicine — only to patients under 18,
- an internist or a doctor with a first-degree specialisation in general medicine — temporarily and on condition of completing the course.

The course requirement for internists and paediatricians will apply from 1 January 2027. The deadline has been postponed several times: originally it was to be 2017, then 2024. Doctors who have provided primary care services for at least 10 years do not have to take the course.

The revenue ceiling is high, too: a full list at a realistic average age coefficient means of the order of <Money value={900000} /> a year from the doctor’s capitation alone, before coordinated care, the delegated budget and consultations. A relatively accessible entry thus meets one of the higher revenue ceilings in the system. A caveat: this is the practice’s revenue, not income — and many primary care doctors are not practice owners, but employees or contractors at ordinary rates.
