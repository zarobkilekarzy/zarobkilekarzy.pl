A visit to a specialist paid for by the National Health Fund (NFZ) costs the patient nothing, but it means waiting: in autumn 2025, on average 4.2 months.[^whc] Those who do not want to wait pay at the private practice next door. This bill is growing faster than prices overall. In March 2026 prices in the “health” category were 4.7% higher than a year earlier, as reported by Polityka Zdrowotna, a health policy news site, and prices of outpatient medical services 7.0% higher. Inflation was 3.0% at the time.[^pz][^gus-iii] This text describes how public money, the waiting list and private prices depend on each other, and what is not known about that relationship.

Over ten years all three lines went up. The NFZ budget grew every year, and so did the price of a private visit — fastest in 2022–2023, when inflation was highest. The waiting time grew more slowly and unevenly: in 2021 it was shorter than before the pandemic, then it grew again, and since 2024 it has been the longest in the history of the Barometer.[^nfz-plany][^bdl][^whc]

The chart shows that these three things are happening at the same time. On its own it does not prove which causes which — three rising lines would look similar if each had its own cause. The following sections show what links them: what the NFZ pays for, who pays privately and why, and why there are few of the doctors whose hours both systems compete for.

## A visit for PLN 231.52

Every year Statistics Poland (GUS) publishes the average price of a visit to a specialist doctor from its retail price surveys. This is the price the patient pays: a visit paid for by the NFZ has no price, so it does not appear in these surveys. In 2015 the average visit cost <Money value={visit(2015)} />, in 2025 <Money value={visit(2025)} />, i.e. {formatEN(visit(2025) / visit(2015), 1)} times more.[^bdl] Prices overall rose by about 57% over that time,[^gus-cpi] so after adjusting for inflation a visit is about 66% more expensive (our own calculation). Its price rose fastest in 2022–2023: by 18.8% and 15.2% a year.

The average does not say how much of the doctor’s time one gets for that price. In October 2026 an online content creator described on social media a private visit for <Money value={280} />: according to his account, the doctor looked at one blood test result, recommended vitamin D and ended the consultation after about two minutes. The post with the receipt was reported by Onet, a Polish news portal.[^onet] This is one person’s account, not a measurement. It does show, however, that the price of a private visit is fixed, while its length depends on the doctor and nobody measures it.

This series ends in 2025. The price indices are more recent. In September 2026 outpatient medical and rehabilitation services — excluding dentistry — were 7.9% more expensive than a year earlier, and prices overall 4.1%.[^gus-ix] Medicines and hospital services are becoming more expensive more slowly, so the “health” category as a whole rose by 4.6%.

The rapid rise in the price of a visit does not necessarily mean that someone is making a disproportionate profit on it. A visit is above all an hour of work by the doctor and the reception staff, and services that consist mainly of work usually become more expensive at the pace of wages, not of goods prices. From 2015 to 2025 the minimum wage rose from <Money value={wage(2015)} /> to <Money value={wage(2025)} />, i.e. {formatEN(wage(2025) / wage(2015), 1)} times — roughly as much as the price of a visit.[^placa] So the question is not only “why is it so expensive”, but also: why are patients paying more and more often when more and more money goes to public healthcare?

## More money, not many more visits

Costs in the NFZ plan more than tripled in 2015–2025, and roughly doubled after inflation. The average waiting time for a service grew by about 43% over that period — we show this in our compilation [The NFZ budget and waiting lists](/en/data/nfz-budget/#money-and-the-waiting-list). What that money bought can be seen in NFZ reports. The longest comparison under a single, unchanged definition covers specialist outpatient care (AOS), i.e. specialist clinics and tests such as CT or MRI scans, in 2022–2024.[^nfz2024]

The figures come from the NFZ report for 2024 and from the WHC Barometer: in November 2022 the average wait for a service was {formatEN(WHC_2022.single, 1)} months, in autumn 2024 {formatEN(WHC_2024.single, 1)} months.[^nfz2024][^whc]

In 2024 the average service cost the NFZ <Money value={Math.round(perService(A2))} />, {formatEN(pct(perService(A0), perService(A2)))}% more than two years earlier. Prices overall rose by about 15% over that time, so even after adjusting for inflation a service became about 19% more expensive (our own calculation). Roughly two thirds of the growth in costs is the higher cost of a single service, and one third is more services. Part of the growth is more expensive diagnostics and pay rises for staff under the act on minimum pay in healthcare, and part is higher hourly rates for doctors’ work. The NFZ report does not separate these components.

The NFZ itself admits that the money has not shortened waiting lists. In February 2025 the Fund said that spending on specialist clinics rose from <Money value={6.2e9} scale="billion" /> in 2018 to <Money value={18.2e9} scale="billion" /> in 2024, while the number of services rose “only slightly” (our translation). The NFZ deputy president judged that the abolition of limits in 2021 had not brought the expected effect of shorter waiting lists.[^nfz-aos] The Supreme Audit Office (NIK) described the same thing in clinics for children: despite higher spending and unit prices, access got worse, and the median waiting time rose in 32 of 36 types of clinic between the end of 2020 and mid-2024.[^nik]

Money can shorten waiting lists. According to OECD comparative studies, the countries that succeeded increased spending permanently rather than once.[^siciliani] The condition, however, is that the money buys more hours of work. When the number of hours grows more slowly than the budget, the extra money goes into the price of an hour. With a fixed pool of money, the number of hours is inversely proportional to the rate — this is arithmetic, not a hypothesis. Why the number of doctors grows slowly is described in our analysis [Why Poland is short of doctors](/en/analysis/why-poland-is-short-of-doctors/), and how the NFZ sets the prices of services in [Pricing of healthcare services](/en/mechanisms/pricing-of-healthcare-services/).

## Fewer places at medical school, a more expensive hour

Money buys a higher price instead of more hours, because specialists’ hours cannot be bought quickly. Their number is still affected by a decision taken three decades ago. According to Rynek Zdrowia, a healthcare news site, the number of first-year places in medicine was cut from more than {formatEN(BEFORE)} in 1987 to {formatEN(BOTTOM)} in full-time studies in 2003.[^rz2019] We know these figures only from the press — we write about this in our analysis [Cutting medical school places](/en/analysis/cutting-medical-school-places/).

The cohorts admitted under the lowest limits are today doctors in mid-career, at the age at which a specialist has the most experience and most often runs their own practice.[^crl] When they are missing, clinics with an NFZ contract, hospitals and private practices compete for the same, smaller pool of hours. By the simple arithmetic of the market, the price of an hour rises on both sides: the NFZ pays more for a similar number of visits, and the patient pays more for a private visit.

Admission limits have been rising again for years: in 2025/26 medicine had 10,504 places.[^limity] From admission to medical school to the title of specialist, however, takes 12–13 years, so the cohorts from the latest admissions will become specialists roughly in 2034–2038. The Ministry of Health and a team working at the Supreme Medical Chamber (NIL) predict that there may then be more doctors than needed. We describe both forecasts in our analysis [Why Poland is short of doctors](/en/analysis/why-poland-is-short-of-doctors/). Until then, the shortage of specialists from the cohorts after the cuts is one of the reasons why extra money raises the price of an hour faster than the number of visits.

## A private visit instead of the waiting list

The simplest way to skip the waiting list is legal: pay. A patient who does not want to wait for an appointment at a clinic with an NFZ contract books the first available slot at a private practice, often with the same specialist. This is how most of those who pay explain using paid care, as shown below.

An extreme example was described in September 2026 by Głos Wielkopolski, a regional daily. A 52-year-old woman from Piła, whose fingers had stiffened after a fracture, was given a referral for reconstruction marked as urgent at a university hospital in Poznań. At registration she was given a date: 1 October 2035, place 3,214 on a list of 6,738 people. For urgent rehabilitation, she says, she would have had to wait up to three years. The hospital did not comment on the case.[^gw] The Greater Poland (Wielkopolska) branch of the NFZ explained on Polsat News that the date depends on medical indications and the facility’s resources, and that there are about 20 hand surgeons in Poland with the international specialist examination. According to the Fund, the wait for procedures that are not life-threatening is on average 1.5–2 years.[^polsat] With so few specialists, extra money will not by itself increase the number of procedures — at most it can raise the price of an hour of those who are there.

Those who wait too long go private — if they can afford it. CBOS, the Public Opinion Research Centre, asks about this regularly. In June 2025, 40% of adults had seen a specialist outside the NFZ in the six months before the survey, paying themselves or through additional insurance. In 2018 the figure was 29%. In both years 40% used specialist visits paid for by the NFZ.[^cbos2025] There were no fewer public visits, but there were more private ones.

CBOS last asked about the reasons in 2023. Among people who used paid care, 79% named shorter waiting times — by far the most common reason. The other reasons were clearly less frequent: 27% of respondents expected better specialists, 19% more efficient service.[^cbos2023]

The demand that the waiting list pushes out of the public system goes to private practices. A patient who is offered an endocrinologist in a year’s time by a clinic with an NFZ contract — on average 12.9 months according to the WHC Barometer — does not spend long comparing prices.[^whc] This does not have to mean collusion or dishonesty: with a shortage of specialists and an urgent need, the price is set by how much patients are willing to pay so as not to wait.

## How much we pay out of pocket

Private visits, medicines, dentistry and tests that patients pay for themselves are counted by GUS in the National Health Account. In 2024 households spent <Money value={bn(OOP_2024)} scale="billion" /> in this way, {formatEN(pct(OOP_2023, OOP_2024))}% more than a year earlier.[^nrz2024] Per inhabitant this is about <Money value={oopPerPerson} /> a year (our own calculation: the GUS amount divided by {formatEN(POPULATION_2024 / 1e6, 1)} million inhabitants).[^ludnosc] Together with medical subscription plans and voluntary insurance, private health spending came to <Money value={bn(PRIVATE_2024)} scale="billion" />.[^nrz2024]

For scale: in 2024 patients spent {formatEN(OOP_2024 / (A2.cost / 1e6), 1)} times more out of their own pockets than the NFZ spent on all specialist clinics and the tests carried out in them (<Money value={bn(A2.cost / 1e6)} scale="billion" />).[^nfz2024] The GUS amount covers everything patients pay for, including medicines and the dentist, so it is not the price of the waiting list for specialists alone. It does show, however, that alongside the public system there is a second one, financed directly by the sick. How this spending has changed since 2020 is shown in our compilation [The NFZ budget and waiting lists](/en/data/nfz-budget/#how-much-we-pay-ourselves).

## The same doctors on both sides

The public and the private waiting lists do not have separate doctors. A doctor who sees patients at a clinic with an NFZ contract may see patients at a private practice after hours, and many work this way. The medical self-government studied this in spring 2026 in a survey of 2,243 doctors and dentists. The sample was not random, so the result does not speak for all doctors, but it shows the scale of the phenomenon.[^nil]

- 30.8% of respondents worked only on publicly funded services,
- 31.0% mainly publicly and occasionally privately,
- 18.5% publicly and privately to a similar extent,
- 5.4% mainly privately, and 11.9% only privately.

In total about 55% combine both sectors. Asked about the problems of such combining, respondents most often named adding patients outside a transparent order (41.4%), the risk of using public resources for private work (35.4%) and moving patients from the public waiting list to private visits (27.3%).[^nil] These are doctors’ own opinions about the risks, not a measurement of how often they occur.

## A ticket through the private practice

A private visit can also shorten the path to treatment paid for by the NFZ. When a doctor sees patients privately and works in a hospital, a paid consultation can become a ticket to a ward — faster than the waiting list. This path has four steps, and most of them are legal:

<NumberedList items={TICKET_STEPS} />

The basis is the provisions of the act on publicly funded healthcare services: a referral to hospital may be issued by a “doctor”, and a referral to a specialist clinic by a “health insurance doctor”.[^art57] The hospital is to admit patients in the order in which they register, taking into account the medical category, which it sets itself.[^art20] How often patients from a private practice get a date sooner than their condition justifies is not known: nobody matches paid visits with admissions funded by the NFZ. In the medical self-government’s survey, adding patients outside a transparent order was the most frequently named problem of combining public and private work (41.4%).[^nil]

Individual inspections are known. In June 2026 the NFZ fined a department (klinika) of one of the Warsaw university hospitals <Money value={289000} />. According to the inspection findings, 70% of patients had been admitted out of order, and 10 people bypassing the waiting list. The inspection was preceded by an anonymous letter from employees, according to which those admitted outside the queue were mainly patients from one doctor’s private practice. The NFZ did not confirm this. The hospital disagrees with the inspection results and says that it admitted patients according to medical criteria.[^interia] The NIK, in its inspection of clinics for children, in turn described a facility that saw patients privately during hours in which, under its NFZ contract, it should have been seeing public patients.[^nik]

A tool to check such cases is to come from the act on pay limits for medical staff, which the Sejm passed on 22 October 2026; it is now before the Senate.[^glosowanie] Facilities with an NFZ contract will have to provide the Fund with information also on services outside that contract.[^druk3157] The NFZ could then check whether patients admitted out of order had previously seen the same doctors privately. The President of the NFZ admitted that today the Fund has no tools to examine links between work in the public system and doctors’ private practice.[^gp-kontrole]

## Ban the combination?

Health economists call a doctor’s work in both sectors a conflict of interest: the longer the public waiting list, the more willingly a patient who can afford it goes private, often to the same doctor.[^siciliani] The simplest answer is to forbid doctors with an NFZ contract to see patients privately. The evidence, however, is not clear-cut. A theoretical model from 1997 shows that when doctors are allowed to see patients privately, the public waiting list may grow.[^iversen] A 2011 review of studies, however, finds that there is little empirical evidence and that it points in different directions.[^socha] Work in two sectors may also give patients more doctor hours than the public system alone would.

Among doctors a ban has little support. In the medical self-government’s survey, a full ban on combining public and private work was supported by 15.1% of respondents (excluding “hard to say” answers). A ban on seeing patients privately in the same scope in which the doctor works for the NFZ was supported by 22.9%, and 67.7% were against. The most support — 71.9% — went to full separation of public and private waiting lists, schedules and billing, but almost as many respondents (68.9%) supported keeping the current freedom without new restrictions.[^nil]

As risks of too strict a separation, doctors named some doctors leaving for private work (74.8%) and longer waiting lists in the public system (58.9%). At the same time, 56.8% said that under predictable and attractive conditions they would choose to work only on public services — most often on condition of guaranteed high pay (67.1%).[^nil] The Presidium of the Supreme Medical Council takes a negative view of the government draft that limits doctors’ total working time: according to the self-government, rigid limits amid staff shortages will reduce the number of services under the NFZ and increase the number of purely private services. At the same time, the self-government declares its support for transparency of contracts.[^nrl]

Other countries choose different paths. Since March 2023 Ireland has employed new consultants in the public health service only on “public only” contracts — a step towards gradually phasing out private practice from public hospitals.[^irl] The United Kingdom does not ban private work, but since 2003 has regulated it in the NHS consultant contract. Work for the NHS takes priority, and the rules cover disclosure of private practice, scheduling, the transfer of patients between the NHS and the private sector and the management of waiting lists. Complying with them is a condition of pay progression.[^nhs]

The available data do not show that a ban alone would shorten waiting lists — with a shortage of specialists it could lengthen them if some doctors chose private practice. The British contract shows another way: not to ban, but to know who works where and how much, and who gets onto the list from a private practice. That knowledge is lacking in Poland today.

## The loop

From these data one can assemble a mechanism in which three trends reinforce one another. This is a hypothesis: the data make it plausible, but none of the available sources proves it.

<Aside title="What these data do not show" variant="note">
Nobody measures how many hours one doctor works under the NFZ and how many privately, or how much they earn in total from all their workplaces. That is why the last link of the loop cannot be checked: whether a higher rate in private practice really raises the cost of an hour at a clinic with an NFZ contract and draws hours away from it. Waiting lists are also lengthened by causes unrelated to money: backlogs after the pandemic, ageing patients, more referrals for tests, and follow-up visits that take appointments away from new patients.
</Aside>

The same act limits the total working time of one person in facilities with an NFZ contract to two full-time posts. We describe it in our texts on [pay limits](/en/analysis/medical-pay-limits-act/) and [multiple jobs](/en/mechanisms/multiple-jobs-parallel-contracts/).
