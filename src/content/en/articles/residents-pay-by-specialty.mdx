This compilation brings together four figures for 29 medical specialties: how much a resident doctor earns during training, how much job advertisements offer per hour in the specialty, how many training places were opened in the latest autumn recruitment and how many doctors applied for one place. Resident pay comes from a regulation and is exact. The rate from advertisements is an estimate based on offers, not on payments.

<Aside title="These are not the earnings of doctors in a given specialty">

Resident pay is the base salary during training — without on-call duty and work elsewhere. The rate in an advertisement is the employer’s proposal, not an amount paid. Doctors often combine several contracts in several facilities (see [Multiple jobs](/en/mechanisms/multiple-jobs-parallel-contracts/)), and nobody publishes the total amounts paid to one person. That is why we call for open data: the total paid to each person from public money, under a permanent identifier, without names ([our proposal](/en/demand/)).

</Aside>

## The 29 specialties compared

The specialties are divided into two groups according to the minister of health’s priority list, because resident pay depends on it. The Centre of Postgraduate Medical Education (CMKP), which runs the recruitment, gave the number of candidates per place only for the most sought-after specialties, and about the least sought-after it wrote that there were more places than applicants. For the others there is a dash.

The last column concerns the end of training: what share of attempts at the test part of the State Specialisation Examination (PES) in 2023–2025 scored at least 60%, i.e. passed the test. Among the specialties in the table, the lowest share is in occupational medicine (**59.6%** of 166 attempts) and the highest in lung diseases (**94.4%** of 195). Repeat attempts are included. The examination also has an oral part, but the Medical Examinations Centre (CEM) does not publish its results, so this is not the share of people who became specialists.[^cem-pes]

## Who is a resident

A resident is a doctor or dentist undergoing specialist training in the residency mode. They have an employment contract with the facility providing the training, concluded for the period required by the specialisation programme.[^uzl-16h] Their base salary, the on-call duty covered by the programme and the contributions are financed by the minister of health from the state budget.[^uzl-16j]

The minister allocates residency places for each qualification procedure. A doctor submits an application in the Training Monitoring System (System Monitorowania Kształcenia) and may list up to 15 options in it: specialty, voivodeship (region) and training mode. The order for residencies is decided by the result of the Medical Final Examination (LEK; for dentists — the Dental Final Examination, LDEK), and a doctorate and scientific publications give extra points.[^uzl-16c]

Not every doctor in specialist training is a resident. In the non-residency mode, doctors train on vacant places outside the minister’s pool — among other things under an employment contract or a civil-law contract with a facility, on paid training leave or with a scholarship funded by a healthcare provider. They do not then receive pay from the minister’s budget, and a civil-law contract for training may not provide for less than the minimum hourly rate.[^uzl-16h]

A resident may also work outside the residency. In specialist outpatient clinics financed by the NFZ, a doctor in specialist training may see patients independently after completing the first year of training — in ophthalmology and in dermatology and venereology after completing the second — once the head of the specialisation confirms their knowledge and skills.[^aos] In job advertisements from 2023, residents were offered on average <Money value={172} /> an hour. Offers for residents after the second or third year, mainly in outpatient clinics, were similar to offers for specialists.[^lop-2023]

## Resident pay: two rates

How much a resident earns is set by the minister of health in a regulation. The act requires this amount to differ by specialty, with particular regard to priority specialties, and by year of training.[^uzl-16j] From {PAY_ACT_EN.from} there are two rates.[^rozp-2026]

The starting point is the statutory minimum pay of a doctor without a specialisation: a coefficient of {formatEN(RESIDENT_COEF, 2)} times the base amount of <Money value={BASE.amount} />, i.e. <Money value={O.first} />.[^prawo-pl] The base amount is the average wage in the economy in the previous year and changes every year on 1 July (see [the pay scale in healthcare](/en/data/other-health-professions-pay/)).[^ustawa-placowa] The amounts in the regulation show that in a priority specialty a resident receives 10% more than this base, and after two years 20% more. In the other specialties pay rises by 3% after two years. The difference between the groups is therefore <Money value={P.first - O.first} /> a month at the start of training and <Money value={P.later - O.later} /> after two years.

### The priority list: 23 specialties

Priority specialties are named in a separate regulation of the minister of health of 27 December 2022. The only later change was the addition of neurology from 1 July 2023.[^priorytety] The list has {PRIORITY_FIELDS.length} items (as of {PRIORITY_AS_OF_EN}), and the regulation on pay repeats the same list.[^rozp-2026]

The table includes {TABLE.priority.fields} specialties from this list. The remaining {PRIORITY_OUTSIDE_TABLE.length} are {priorityOutside} — paediatric and oncological specialties and a specialty for dentists.

### Supplement for a commitment to work in Poland

Every resident, not only in a priority specialty, may declare that after obtaining the specialisation they will work in Poland for a total of {COMMITMENT.years} years out of the following {COMMITMENT.window}, at least full-time, in a facility providing services financed from public funds. In return their base salary rises by <Money value={COMMITMENT.priority} /> a month in a priority specialty and by <Money value={COMMITMENT.other} /> in the others.[^uzl-2b] With the supplement, a resident in a priority specialty receives <Money value={P.first + COMMITMENT.priority} /> in the first two years, and in the other specialties — <Money value={O.first + COMMITMENT.other} />. The supplement is colloquially called the “patriotic voucher” (*bon patriotyczny*).

If the doctor does not keep the commitment or breaks off the specialisation programme, the voivode (the government’s representative in the region) imposes a penalty equal to {formatEN(COMMITMENT.penaltyShare * 100)}% of the total supplements received. The penalty decreases in proportion to the period the doctor has worked in line with the declaration. Anyone who received the supplement for less time than the specialisation lasted has a proportionally shorter commitment, and the supplement can be given up — pay then falls from the following month.[^uzl-2l] An example: a resident in a priority specialty who received the supplement for 60 months and did not work a single day under the commitment will pay <Money value={COMMITMENT.penaltyShare * 60 * COMMITMENT.priority} />.

## Candidates and rates in job advertisements

None of the ten most sought-after specialties named by the director of the CMKP is on the priority list. The table includes {top.length} of them (candidates per place in brackets): {topList}.[^filipiak] Together they had {count(topRez, 'residency place', 'residency places')} — less than half the pool for paediatrics alone ({formatEN(spec('pediatria').rez)}).[^cmkp-miejsca]

At the other end, the CMKP named ten specialties in which there were more places than applicants. Seven of them are on the priority list — in the table {belowPriority}, outside the table radiation oncology. Three are not on the list: {belowOther}, nuclear medicine and nephrology.[^filipiak]

Rates in advertisements follow a similar pattern, though with exceptions. In 2023 the middle specialty outside the list had an average of <Money value={RATE_MEDIAN.other} decimals={0} /> an hour in advertisements, and the middle priority specialty — <Money value={RATE_MEDIAN.priority} decimals={0} /> an hour (the median of the average rates of the specialties in the table).[^lop-2023] The highest average, however, was for psychiatry, which is on the list: <Money value={spec('psychiatria').rate} decimals={0} /> an hour. The report’s authors note that it was pushed up by offers from one network of facilities at PLN 400 an hour. The lowest was for medical rehabilitation, which is not on the list: <Money value={spec('rehabilitacja-medyczna').rate} decimals={0} /> an hour. Lung diseases (<Money value={spec('choroby-pluc').rate} decimals={0} /> an hour) and occupational medicine (<Money value={spec('medycyna-pracy').rate} decimals={0} /> an hour) had higher rates than ophthalmology or gastroenterology, and they are not among the most sought-after specialties. For plastic surgery, which had the most candidates, the report gives no rate.

The table shows a coincidence, not a cause. Higher pay in a priority specialty — by <Money value={P.first - O.first} /> a month at the start of training — does not reverse the proportions: the most candidates per place are in specialties outside the list. The data do not say, however, what decides the choice. They do not cover, for example, working conditions after the specialisation or how much doctors in a given specialty earn in total.

## Specialist training places

For the autumn 2025 session the CMKP listed {count(AUTUMN_2025.rez, 'residency place', 'residency places')} and {count(AUTUMN_2025.nonRez, 'non-residency place', 'non-residency places')}.[^cmkp-miejsca] Doctors submitted {count(AUTUMN_2025.applications, 'formally correct application', 'formally correct applications')}. After two stages, {formatEN(AUTUMN_2025.takenRez + AUTUMN_2025.takenNonRez)} doctors confirmed that they had accepted a place: {formatEN(AUTUMN_2025.takenRez)} in a residency and {formatEN(AUTUMN_2025.takenNonRez)} in the non-residency mode.[^cmkp-wyniki] So {pct(AUTUMN_2025.takenRez, AUTUMN_2025.rez)}% of residencies and {pct(AUTUMN_2025.takenNonRez, AUTUMN_2025.nonRez)}% of non-residency places were taken. In an additional, third stage — only for residencies in priority specialties — another {formatEN(AUTUMN_2025.thirdStageRez)} doctors accepted a place.[^filipiak]

After two stages, {formatEN(AUTUMN_2025.applications - AUTUMN_2025.takenRez - AUTUMN_2025.takenNonRez)} applicants had not confirmed accepting any place, and {formatEN(AUTUMN_2025.rez - AUTUMN_2025.takenRez)} residencies remained vacant. The pool of residencies is concentrated in priority specialties: in the table they have {formatEN(TABLE.priority.rez)} of the {formatEN(tableRez)} residency places ({pct(TABLE.priority.rez, tableRez)}%). The largest pools were only partly filled. Among the ten specialties for which the CMKP gave the number of doctors accepted, almost all residencies were taken in three specialties outside the priority list and in anaesthesiology.

## The priority list and forecasts of shortages

In its health needs maps, the Ministry of Health compares how many doctors will complete a specialisation in a given specialty with the number of those who will reach retirement age. By 2028 it forecasts the largest gap in internal medicine and general surgery. There is also to be a shortfall in specialties outside the priority list: occupational medicine, medical rehabilitation, lung diseases, balneology and otorhinolaryngology. In family medicine and paediatrics, which are on the list, more new specialists are expected than will reach retirement age.[^mapy]
