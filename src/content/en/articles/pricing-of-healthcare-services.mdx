Before the National Health Fund (NFZ) pays for treatment, someone has to set its price. This was meant to be done by an independent agency calculating actual costs. In practice most prices are set by the payer itself, and the price misses the cost in both directions: sometimes the hospital loses money on the patient and sends them to the waiting list, sometimes a procedure becomes a source of profit.

Pricing treatment is genuinely difficult, and we do not accuse anyone of bad faith. We describe a process that was meant to be independent and based on costs, and for the most part is not. The consequences — waiting lists where too little is paid and excess where too much is paid — are borne by patients and taxpayers.

## Who sets the price

Since 2015 the prices of services have been calculated by a separate institution — the Agency for Health Technology Assessment and Tariff System (AOTMiT). The separation was deliberate: one body calculates the cost, another pays, so that the buyer does not decide on its own how much it will pay. The agency collects cost data from facilities, calculates the tariff from them and announces it in a notice. The NFZ then transfers it into its orders and contracts[^taryfy-2023] — in one of the recent changes this meant 11 orders and about 35,000 contract annexes, the NFZ explains.[^aneksy]

According to the Supreme Audit Office (NIK), this division worked mainly on paper.[^nik] In 2015–2020 the independent tariff covered about 17% of the value of NFZ services, and tariff-setting plans were carried out in 32.6%. The remaining prices were set by the payer: since 2017 the President of the NFZ raised prices a dozen or so times — in the Office’s assessment without a reliable analysis of costs, and in some cases without the authority to do so, even changing tariffs set by the Agency. There was also no single standard of cost accounting for all hospitals, so even the data from which the tariff is built were calculated in different ways. These are the findings and assessment of the Office, not a court judgment.

Since the price is only loosely tied to the cost, it drifts away from it — in both directions. How the tariff turns into a transfer to the hospital is described in our text on [how the NFZ pays hospitals](/en/mechanisms/nfz-payment-system/).

## Too low: the hospital pays extra for treatment

When a procedure is priced below its cost, the hospital loses money on every patient — so it admits fewer of them, and the waiting list grows. In 2018 the minister of health admitted that only about 20% of JGP groups (Diagnosis-Related Groups) had been correctly priced, and announced that the others would be recalculated. The trigger was an audit of several institutes, including the oncology institute: they were failing to balance their budgets not because of bad management but because of underpriced services, Rzeczpospolita reported.[^rp-2018]

The underpriced procedures named at the time included cataract operations (a wait of more than a year and a half even in urgent cases), treatment of acute coronary syndromes and unstable angina, and planned unblocking of arteries. A price that is too low does not treat anyone; it only lengthens the waiting list. Someone covers the difference anyway — it comes back as [hospital debt](/en/data/hospital-debt/).

## Too high: the procedure attracts facilities

The gap also works the other way. A procedure priced above its cost “pays” better than others, and facilities are more willing to perform it. The payer itself admits this: defending the changes in surgery in 2024, the NFZ and AOTMiT calculated that funding rises by <Money value={1180000000} scale="billion" decimals={2} /> a year (about 20%) and that 77 of 83 groups have a higher price. In their statement they called this plainly profits for hospitals.[^stanowisko]

In the same package the Association of Polish Surgeons (Towarzystwo Chirurgów Polskich) warned that some procedures had been cut below cost: colorectal resection by about 27%, some endoscopic procedures by about 50%.[^chirurdzy] Both sides are right at the same time: the average increase hides local cuts. The price does not result from a hard measurement of cost, but from dispute and negotiation.

This is seen most clearly in an older example. From January 2014 the NFZ lowered the price of cataract operations — by about 30%, depending on the type of procedure. In the first two months of the year the number of procedures rose, while their total value fell:[^zacma]

<Aside title="To be fair: there is no ideal price">

A high or low price is not in itself an abuse, and the real cost of treatment is hard to measure — patients and complications differ. We do not claim that everything can be priced perfectly. We claim something narrower: the process should be independent and verifiable, and for the most part it is not. We describe the mechanism, not specific decisions or people.

</Aside>

## Waiting lists depend on more than the price

The cataract story has a sequel, which shows that a waiting list depends on more than the price of a single procedure. In 2015–2017 some patients waiting for cataract surgery went abroad — 93% of them to Czechia. The EU directive on cross-border healthcare allows this: the patient pays the foreign clinic, and the NFZ reimburses them up to the amount it would have paid for the same service in Poland.[^usoz-42c] The reimbursement is therefore calculated according to the Polish price. In 2017 patients submitted **14,433** reimbursement claims for procedures in Czechia, worth more than <Money value={30900000} scale="million" decimals={1} />.[^zacma-wyjazdy]

The NFZ then increased the value of its cataract contracts, that is, the pool from which it buys procedures: by 4% in 2016 and by more than 30% in 2017. Between 2015 and 2017 the number of procedures in Poland rose by **73,192**, and by the end of 2017 the waiting list was shorter by more than 40,000 people (compared with January 2015). Yet the trips abroad did not decline. The authors of the analysis point to one of the reasons: abroad the patient could pay extra for a better lens, while in Poland the price and the regulations in practice did not allow this.[^zacma-wyjazdy] More money shortened the waiting list for one procedure, but it was the price, together with the regulations, that determined which lens a patient would get at home. The NFZ started funding the toric lens, which corrects astigmatism, only from July 2018.[^zacma-wyjazdy]

## When the tariff chases wages rather than costs

There is one more track on which pricing comes loose from the cost of the procedure — because it serves a different purpose. The act on the minimum pay in healthcare, as worded in 2022, requires minimum salaries, calculated from the average wage in the economy, to be raised every year. So that hospitals have the money to pay them, the President of AOTMiT recommends raising prices every year. According to the 2026 recommendation, tariff increases to cover statutory pay will raise NFZ spending by about <Money value={9250000000} scale="billion" decimals={2} /> over 12 months: from 1 July the minimum for a specialist is <Money value={12910} />, and for a doctor without a specialisation <Money value={10595} /> gross.[^rp-2026]

The rises themselves follow from the act — this is the law, not an abuse. The problem lies in the mechanism. Rising pay has to be covered from somewhere, so prices are raised. Higher prices go to hospitals and are mostly spent on the same pay. And because the minimum rises every year, the tariff rises every year too. Pricing stops answering the question of how much a procedure costs and starts answering the question of how much has to be added to salaries.

Politically this is convenient, because the rises do not require reaching into the state budget. But it blurs the only signal by which one could tell where we pay too little and where too much. That this is a stopgap is shown by the fate of an earlier solution: the so-called 16% mechanism of 2022 was phased out after a year.[^mechanizm-16] The cost of the pay act is about one third of the NFZ plan — we show it in our overview of the [NFZ budget](/en/data/nfz-budget/).

Price can also reach pay by a shorter route: through the doctor’s contract with the hospital. Auditing 22 hospitals in 2011–2014, NIK described contracts in which pay was calculated as an hourly rate, as a commission on services paid for by the NFZ, or as both. The amount of the commission depended on how the NFZ priced the procedure and on the supply of specialists, and the hospital paid it regardless of its own costs. The Office called this “a kind of privatisation of NFZ contracts” and noted that even highly priced procedures did not guarantee the hospital a profit.[^nik-kontrakty]

This model also appeared later. In a 2019 call for offers, a clinical hospital in Lublin allowed a rate for an outpatient-clinic doctor of **up to 60% of the value of the procedure** under its NFZ contract, reduced by the cost of tests ordered.[^konkurs-60] This is the ceiling in the call, not proof of how much anyone actually received or of how common the model is. Under such a contract every change in the price immediately changes the doctor’s pay, while the hospital still bears the rest of the cost of treatment. We describe contract models in our text on [contract vs employment](/en/mechanisms/contract-vs-employment/).
