Spain spends a smaller share of its national income on health than the EU average, and its inhabitants live the longest of the countries we describe. The National Health System is financed from taxes, free at the point of treatment and managed entirely by the regions. We describe how it works according to Spanish acts and health ministry data, and check what is public in it. Waiting lists for surgery and for specialists are published every six months for each region. Also public by law are the permits a public doctor needs in order to work privately — doctors’ earnings are not.

## Seventeen systems in one

The National Health System (*Sistema Nacional de Salud*) covers all residents and has no separate contribution — the money comes from taxes. The health ministry sets the common rules, but treatment is planned, organised and paid for by 17 autonomous communities, each through its own health service.[^profil]

The communities account for 93.6% of public health expenditure. The largest item is staff pay — 44.8% — because most hospitals and health centres are the regional health services’ own units. Purchasing services from private entities took 10.0% of spending, and primary care 14.4%.[^egsp]

State civil servants, judges and the military remain outside the system. They have their own mutual insurance societies and choose every year whether they want to be treated in the public health service or by a private insurer at the state’s expense. The agreement with the insurers for 2025–2027 costs the budget €4,808.5 million and covers more than 1.5 million people; the government changed the terms of the tender and raised the rates by 41.2%.[^muface]

## What it costs

<CountryBars title="Current health expenditure in 2024 (Eurostat)" codes={['ES', 'PL', 'EU']} keys={['healthGdp', 'perCapitaEur', 'perCapitaPps', 'publicShare', 'oopShare']} />

Although visits and hospitals are free of charge, patients cover more than one fifth of spending out of pocket — more than in Poland.[^eurostat-sha] According to the profile by the OECD and the European Commission, this is mainly the dentist, whom the public system hardly finances at all, and medicines.[^profil] According to insurers’ data, 12.8 million people have a private policy.[^polisy]

## A budget instead of a price list

In most regions a hospital or health centre does not bill the payer for a service. Public facilities receive an annual budget, and doctors a fixed salary.[^hit-es] The communities can also buy treatment from private entities under agreements.[^ley14]

## What the patient pays

A visit to a doctor and a stay in hospital are free of charge. The patient contributes to the cost of prescription medicines — the more, the higher their income. Since May 2026 a working person pays from 40 to 60% of the price of a medicine, and a pensioner as a rule 10%; the co-payments of people on lower incomes and of pensioners are limited by a monthly cap, from €8.23 to €61.75.[^copago]

It is the family doctor who refers the patient to a specialist and to hospital.[^profil]

## Doctors’ work and pay

Doctors in the public health service have a status similar to that of civil servants. Their rights and duties are set out in the act on the statute of health service staff,[^estatuto-ley] and basic pay is the same across the country and follows from the budget act; each health service sets the supplements and on-call rates itself and publishes them in tables. In the Balearic Islands a specialist earns €54,413.14 a year in 2026, excluding on-call duty, seniority supplements and professional development supplements.[^baleary] On-call duty is paid separately: an hour of a specialist’s on-call duty on a working day costs €33.12 in the Balearic Islands and €28.49 in Madrid.[^baleary] [^madryt-dyzury]

According to the OECD, a salaried specialist in Spain earned 2.66 times as much as the average worker (2024, estimate).[^oecd-remun] We found no official statistics on doctors’ average earnings.

In 2026 doctors’ unions organised strikes, demanding a separate statute for doctors.[^strajk]

## Public and private work at the same time

A doctor in the public health service who also wants to work privately needs a permit. The 1984 act on incompatibilities requires such permits to be entered in the staff register and prohibits private activity connected with the doctor’s own unit, and the act on the statute of health service staff treats work without a permit as a very serious offence, punishable by suspension for a period of two to six years or removal from the service.[^incompat] [^estatuto-ley]

This is a rare case in which combining public and private work is not only regulated but also public — more on that in the next section. How many doctors actually work in both sectors is not known: we found no such figure.

## What is public

**Permits for private work.** The 2013 transparency act requires the administration to publish decisions granting permission to combine public work with another activity.[^ley19] The Community of Madrid has published lists of such permits by name on its transparency portal since January 2015 — for all its employees, doctors included.[^madryt-portal] The file of 5 November 2026 gives the surname, the position, the hospital, the date of the decision, a short description of the second activity and whether it is carried out on the person’s own account or for someone else; there is no separate column with the name of the private entity or with the specialty.[^madryt-portal] In 2023 the authority released, at a citizen’s request, a list with the specialty and the name of the private entity. It refused to indicate which permits are still current — a doctor has no duty to report that they have stopped using one.[^madryt-zgody]

**Waiting lists — every six months, region by region.** The ministry publishes the number of people waiting and the average waiting time. At the end of 2025, 853,740 people were waiting for surgery, 122 days on average, and 21.6% had been waiting more than six months. The average wait for a first visit to a specialist was 102 days.[^sisle]

The ministry notes that the communities are responsible for the reliability of the data and that they count waiting lists according to their own criteria, so the differences between regions result partly from the method.[^sisle]

**Pay tables.** Each health service publishes its pay tables and on-call rates.[^baleary] [^madryt-dyzury]

**Management pay.** The transparency act requires the annual pay of people in the highest positions to be published.[^ley19] The pay of an ordinary doctor is protected by personal data rules: according to joint guidance of the transparency council and the data protection agency, pay by name can be obtained on request only for managerial posts and posts filled by discretionary appointment.[^criterio]

**Industry payments — by name.** Pharmaceutical companies have published these payments since 2016, and today — according to the industry organisation — entirely by name. This is a rule of the industry’s code, not of an act. For 2025 the companies reported €833 million, of which €453 million went to research and development.[^farma]

## How many doctors there are and how long people wait

According to a Eurostat estimate, Spain has 464 practising doctors per 100,000 inhabitants — one fifth more than Poland.[^eurostat-lekarze] Entry to specialist training is through a nationwide examination; 9,676 places for doctors are planned for 2027, about 4% more than a year earlier.[^mir]

The wait for a visit to a family doctor — when it could not be booked for the same or the next day — averaged 9.15 days in 2025: 4.91 in the Basque Country, 11.16 in Andalusia.[^barometro]

Despite long waiting lists for surgery, few Spaniards give up a visit because of cost, distance or waiting: 1.6% compared with 2.9% of Poles. The reason is almost always the waiting list, almost never the price.[^eurostat-silc]

Life expectancy in Spain is the highest among the countries we describe; the EU average is 81.5 years, and the EU averages 86.8 deaths from treatable causes per 100,000 inhabitants.[^wyniki] We write about the opinion surveys [separately](/en/analysis/satisfaction-with-healthcare-in-poland/).

## Professional and criminal liability

**Professional.** A doctor in the public health service is subject to disciplinary liability before the employer. The act on the statute of health service staff lists very serious offences — including gross failure to perform duties, abandonment of service and additional work without a permit — and penalties: from a reprimand, through suspension for a period of two to six years, to removal from the service.[^estatuto-ley] How many such proceedings are under way is not known. We found no statistics from the medical chambers either. For comparison: [how it works in Poland](/en/analysis/doctor-discipline-licence-revocation/).

**Criminal.** The criminal code provides for one to four years’ imprisonment for causing death through gross negligence. If the negligence was professional in nature, the court additionally imposes a ban on practising the profession for a period of three to six years. Less serious negligence is punishable by a fine. A separate offence is refusal of assistance or abandonment of service by a person professionally obliged to provide treatment, where this exposes the patient to serious danger.[^kk]

We found no official statistics on convictions of doctors.

**Compensation.** A patient treated in the public health service does not sue the doctor but the administration. Only after compensation has been paid can the administration seek reimbursement from the employee — and only if the employee acted intentionally or was guilty of gross negligence.[^ley40] Spain has no system of payments without establishing fault; how such a system works in other countries is described in the text on [compensation without establishing fault](/en/mechanisms/no-fault-compensation-and-doctors-liability/).

## Method

We base the description of the system on the texts of Spanish acts from the official gazette (BOE), the health ministry’s statistics on public health expenditure and its waiting-list information system, ministry press releases, the pay tables of regional health services, the guidance of the transparency council and the data protection agency, Eurostat data, and the country profile prepared by the OECD and the European Observatory on Health Systems and Policies for the European Commission. Secondary sources — the trade press and data from the pharmaceutical companies’ organisation — are marked in the footnotes.

The Spanish statistics on public expenditure (6.4% of GDP) cover only public money and are calculated differently from the Eurostat data (9.25% of GDP, public and private spending together) — for comparisons with Poland we use Eurostat only.
