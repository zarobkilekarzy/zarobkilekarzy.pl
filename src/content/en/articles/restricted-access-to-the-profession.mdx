How many new doctors there will be, and in which fields, is not decided by a single institution. Between the secondary-school leaving exam and the title of specialist there are at least four gates: medical school, the licence to practise, a specialisation place and a supervisor who will oversee the training. Each is guarded by someone else, with a different instrument.

This text describes those gates — who sets them and on what basis. Why doctors are in short supply despite growing limits, and how many specialisation places remain empty, we show in our analysis [Why Poland is short of doctors](/en/analysis/why-poland-is-short-of-doctors/).

## Who guards which door

Most of the rules are laid down in the Act on the professions of doctor and dentist, and the rules for creating study programmes in the Law on higher education.[^ustawa][^pka] The table shows who decides at successive stages.

In this arrangement the medical self-government has no right of veto over the limits. It gives opinions on draft regulations, and by virtue of the act it acts in two places: regional medical councils grant the licence to practise to people who meet the statutory conditions, and the Supreme Medical Council (NRL) proposes one of the five members of the teams assessing facilities that want to train specialists.[^ustawa]

## Medical school: the minister of health sets the limit

How many people may begin studying medicine is set every year by the minister of health in a regulation, separately for each university. For the 2026/27 academic year it is 10,730 places, 226 more than a year earlier, when the limit was 10,504.[^lim2026][^lim2025] The limit is an upper bound on admissions, not the number of people admitted. How it has grown over the last decade and why more places do not immediately mean more doctors, we describe in our analysis [Why Poland is short of doctors](/en/analysis/why-poland-is-short-of-doctors/).

In June 2026 the deputy minister of health said that the limit no longer needs to be increased and that in the coming years it may even fall.[^mz-limity] There is no shortage of applicants: for medicine at Adam Mickiewicz University in Poznań, the July 2026 recruitment had more than 22 candidates per place — the most of any programme at that university.[^uam] This is the result of one university, not a national average.

### New programmes: the minister’s permission and the PKA opinion

Since an amendment of November 2021, doctors may also be educated by universities that do not have academic status. Permission for a new programme is issued by the minister responsible for higher education, and the quality of education is assessed by the Polish Accreditation Committee (PKA). Its opinion was not binding on the minister: in 2022–2023 17 universities received permission, 11 of them without a positive PKA opinion, and nine started teaching despite a negative opinion.[^pka] These are numbers of universities, not of programmes.

The second gate is the limit set by the minister of health. In the regulation for 2024/25 nine universities received no places,[^pka] and in the draft for 2025/26 the ministry left out six universities without a positive PKA opinion.[^limity-2025] Whether the new programmes educate worse than the older ones, we examine in our analysis [Who drops out of medicine and who leaves the country](/en/analysis/medical-school-dropouts-and-emigration/).

### The position of the self-government

The medical self-government opposes increasing admissions outside the universities it considers proven. In June 2025 the Presidium of the Supreme Medical Council criticised the draft limit, which provided for 6,649 places on full-time medical programmes taught in Polish, about 200 more than a year earlier. It cited concern for the standard of education and patient safety, an expert report commissioned by the chamber according to which the number of doctors will exceed the demand for services in 2032, and the claim that waiting lists result largely from the inefficiency of the system.[^pnrl-limity]

In June 2026, after reports of possible corruption in the issuing of PKA opinions on new programmes, the Presidium demanded the replacement of the committee’s entire membership and the re-accreditation of all medical universities, especially newly opened ones.[^pnrl-pka] The argument about quality is supported by negative opinions of the PKA itself.[^pka] The forecast of a surplus of doctors, on the other hand, is a claim of the chamber’s expert report. How it compares with the ministry’s forecast and with data on waiting lists, we describe in our analysis [Why Poland is short of doctors](/en/analysis/why-poland-is-short-of-doctors/).

The self-government has not always spoken of a surplus. In October 2019 the vice-president of the Supreme Medical Council estimated in a press interview that Poland was short of about 68,000 doctors. As the basis he gave a recalculation of data from the Central Register of Doctors, without describing the method. The then minister of health spoke of “a methodology unknown to me” (our translation), although he admitted that there were too few doctors; the vice-president himself also referred to the shortfall of 50,000 officially cited at the time.[^nrl-2019] The same text gave a ratio of 2.4 doctors per 1,000 population. It came from GUS data for 2010, calculated using the old method.[^gus-metoda] According to the OECD, in 2023 Poland had 3.9 practising doctors per 1,000 population, compared with an EU average of 4.3.[^soh] We describe the definitions and the change in counting method in our analysis [Why Poland is short of doctors](/en/analysis/why-poland-is-short-of-doctors/).

## Specialisation: three limits on one place

After the postgraduate internship and the Medical Final Examination (LEK), a doctor applies for a specialisation. Here the number of places is determined by three limits, set by different institutions:

1. **The pool of residencies.** Residency places in particular fields are allocated by the minister of health, who also pays the resident’s salary from the state budget. The remaining vacant places in accredited facilities are non-residency places — the doctor’s work is then paid for by the facility.[^ustawa]
2. **Accreditation of the facility.** Only a unit entered on the list of the director of the Centre of Postgraduate Medical Education (CMKP) may provide training. It must have, among other things, a ward of the right profile, equipment, a sufficient number of services and at least one specialist who agrees to be a specialisation supervisor. The application is assessed by a five-member team: the chair and one member are proposed by the national consultant, one each by the scientific society and the Supreme Medical Council, and the fifth member is a representative of the consultant. The director of the CMKP enters the facility on the list and sets its maximum number of training places for five years.[^ustawa]
3. **The specialisation supervisor.** Every doctor in training has a supervisor with a second-degree specialisation or the title of specialist in the field. One supervisor may oversee at most three doctors at a time, and in cases justified by staffing needs, with the consent of the national consultant, four. The same limit applies to an internship supervisor, and a specialisation supervisor may additionally oversee a specialty placement of at most two doctors. For supervision the supervisor receives an allowance: <Money value={500} /> a month for one doctor and <Money value={1000} /> for more than one, from the voivode’s budget.[^ustawa]

A facility therefore cannot have more doctors in training at once than four times the number of its specialisation supervisors, nor more than its accreditation allows. The act also provides for adjustments in both directions: when a facility with vacant places does not admit a doctor referred to it, the voivode (the government’s regional representative) applies for its limit to be reduced, and when a field lacks training places, the minister of health may ask a hospital to apply for accreditation.[^ustawa] How often the limit of supervisors or of accreditation actually closes the path to specialisation cannot be checked in the data we have been able to access.

Overall there are more places than applicants, and the places that remain empty are mainly non-residency ones: in spring 2026 nobody confirmed admission to 52% of residency places and 89% of non-residency places.[^cmkp26] We describe successive sessions and the reasons for this difference in our analysis [Why Poland is short of doctors](/en/analysis/why-poland-is-short-of-doctors/). In the fields for which candidates compete most fiercely, entry is therefore decided by the pool of residencies, the places in accredited facilities and the ranking.

At the end of the training there is no longer any limit on places. The written part of the State Specialisation Examination (PES) is passed by everyone who scores at least 60% of the points,[^ustawa] and in 2016–2025 between **73.0%** and **80.6%** of attempts passed it each year.[^pes] The Medical Examinations Centre (CEM) does not publish the results of the oral part.

## The ranking: what counts is the percentage of the maximum

When there are more applicants than places, the order is set by a competitive procedure. Its result is a percentage of the maximum number of points. For residency, the LEK score counts together with additional points for a doctorate and publications, and in the non-residency procedure also, among other things, for three years of work in an accredited facility. With equal scores, the average grade from medical school decides, and then the time of submitting the application. In the application one may indicate up to 15 options: field, voivodeship and mode of training.[^ustawa]

From the lists of those qualified after the spring 2025 session, we read off, for each voivodeship and field, the lowest score of a person who was given a residency. A score of at least 90% occurred in 34 of 533 such pairs, i.e. in about 6% of them, and the median was about 81%.[^listy] The highest thresholds were in endocrinology, dermatology and plastic surgery.

## A diploma from outside the European Union

A doctor with a diploma from outside the EU usually has to obtain recognition of the diploma in Poland or pass the Medical Verification Examination (LEW), demonstrate knowledge of Polish, complete an internship and pass the LEK. The licence to practise is then granted by a regional medical council.[^ustawa]

Alongside this path, the act has a simplified procedure. The minister of health may — after consulting, if the minister considers it necessary, a national or regional consultant — agree that a specialist with a diploma from outside the EU may work within a specified scope, period and place — in the hospital that employs them. The conditions include three years of experience as a specialist, legalised diplomas and a declaration of knowledge of Polish; recognition of the diploma and the LEW and LEK examinations are not among the conditions. On the basis of the minister’s decision, the chamber grants a licence to practise for at most five years, with no possibility of extension, and for the first year the doctor works under the supervision of a mentor. A second procedure, the conditional licence to practise, is permitted by the act in a state of epidemic threat or epidemic.[^ustawa]

In March 2025 the Supreme Medical Council demanded the repeal of both simplified procedures.[^nrl-uproszczone] Since 5 March 2026 the separate path under the act on assistance to citizens of Ukraine, including the minister’s consent to practise as a doctor, has ceased to operate. Doctors who used it are now covered by the provisions on the conditional licence to practise.[^ukr] The simplified procedure under the Act on the professions of doctor remained in the text of the act as of June 2026.[^ustawa] A 2026 draft by the ministry provides for applications under this procedure to be accepted only until the end of 2026, and from 1 May 2026 doctors from the simplified paths had to demonstrate knowledge of Polish at B1 level or their licence to practise would expire. We describe this path into the profession, with figures and sources, in our analysis [Doctors from outside the EU](/en/analysis/doctors-from-outside-the-eu/).

## Does restricting entry raise earnings

The debate often features the claim that fewer doctors means higher rates. In this form there is no evidence for it in Poland. The economic literature formulates it more narrowly. According to a Becker Friedman Institute brief based on a 2026 paper by Gottlieb and Nicholson, limits on places at medical schools and in residencies in the USA — especially in the best-paid specialties — ration entry to the profession and allow those who obtain these places to earn high incomes. The authors refer to a 1945 paper by Friedman and Kuznets, which compared the incomes of doctors and dentists.[^bfi] This is a description of a rent for those who are already in, in the American market.

Cross-country comparisons are less clear-cut. According to an OECD report on doctors’ remuneration in 14 countries, differences in the number of general practitioners per population do not seem to have a large effect on differences in their earnings. In Czechia and Finland there were few general practitioners, and they earned relatively little.[^oecd41]

In Poland a coincidence can be seen: the fields with the fewest residencies have the highest thresholds and the most candidates per place. Whether this translates into higher earnings for these specialists remains a hypothesis — a coincidence is not proof of cause. We show the rates from job advertisements in particular fields [by specialty](/en/data/residents-pay-by-specialty/), and other explanations of high contract rates, including the shift to civil-law contracts and pay as a percentage of NFZ revenue, in our analysis [Why Poland is short of doctors](/en/analysis/why-poland-is-short-of-doctors/).

Nor can the effect on patients be attributed to a single mechanism. In 2024, 5.2% of people in Poland who needed medical care did not receive it because of cost, waiting time or distance; the EU average for this indicator was 3.6% (2023).[^soh]
