Amounts quoted as doctors’ pay in different countries often mean different things: sometimes a statutory minimum, sometimes a rate from a pay table, sometimes an average from statistics. That is why we compare pay here with a single measure that the OECD collects for many countries: how many times more than the average worker a specialist doctor earns.

The rest of the text addresses a question that such comparisons usually miss: how Poland, Czechia, Germany, Austria and Sweden pay their doctors and what is publicly known about that pay. We describe each country using the same template — who pays for treatment, how the doctor is employed, what is public and how we know it. At the end we compare the number of doctors per population, counted the same way as in our analysis [Why Poland is short of doctors](/en/analysis/why-poland-is-short-of-doctors/).

## Pay with one measure: a multiple of the average wage

The OECD compares doctors’ pay with the average wage of a full-time employee in the same country. The ratio does not depend on exchange rates and shows how the profession ranks against other earnings. According to *Health at a Glance 2025*, a salaried specialist earned 3.2 times as much as the average worker in Germany, 2.9 times in Poland, 2.5 in Czechia and 2.2 times in Sweden. The average of the 31 OECD countries with such data is 2.8. The data refer to 2023, and for Poland to 2022.[^oecd-pensje]

Poland appears in this comparison only through its salaried doctors. OECD data do not include earnings from contracts, as Demagog, a Polish fact-checking organisation, pointed out when checking a claim about Polish doctors’ pay compared with the rest of Europe.[^demagog] This is a large gap: according to an AOTMiT working document from February 2026, 73% of specialists work under civil-law contracts, mostly as contractors.[^aotmit] OECD data do not show how much the average Polish specialist earns once contracts are counted. We show median payments under contracts and employment in [the pay distribution](/en/data/doctor-pay-distribution/), and the differences between forms of employment in [Contract or employment](/en/mechanisms/contract-vs-employment/).

For Austria the OECD has no data on salaried specialists, only on self-employed doctors: their income in 2021 was 4.6 times the average wage. In Germany the same group stood at 4.9 in 2023.[^oecd-pensje] This is practice income after costs and before tax and contributions, not a salary — which is why we do not put it on the chart next to salaried doctors.

The measure also has limits, which the OECD itself lists. Salaries may be underestimated because some countries exclude overtime, bonuses and income from private practice, and some report data for the public sector only.[^oecd-pensje] Nor does the ratio to the average wage say what the pay can buy: in US dollars adjusted for purchasing power parity, which the OECD gives in the same dataset, a salaried specialist earned more in Sweden than in Poland.[^oecd-pensje]

## What is public in the five countries

Pay transparency is not all or nothing. In each of the five countries part of doctors’ pay is publicly known and part is not. The countries differ in what is public: only the minimum, a full table of rates, statistical averages or the pay of a specific person. The table orders them from the narrowest to the broadest scope of transparency; details and sources are in the country sections below.

None of these countries publishes, of its own accord, how much public money one person received in total from all their workplaces. Outside Europe the picture is similar: Taiwan shows averages for the profession and hospital reports ([our analysis of Taiwan](/en/analysis/taiwan-healthcare-transparency/)), and two Canadian provinces publish annual payments to individual doctors, but each only from one insurance plan ([our analysis of Canada](/en/analysis/canada-doctors-special-legislation/)).

## Poland: a public minimum, hidden contracts

Treatment in the public system is paid for by a single payer — the National Health Fund (NFZ), financed mainly by the health contribution. A hospital doctor works either as an employee or under a civil-law contract, most often as a contractor running their own business; as noted above, this is how most specialists work.

There is no pay table for doctors. A 2017 act sets only the minimum base salary for employees: from 1 July 2026 a specialist must receive at least <Money value={12910.16} /> gross a month, excluding on-call duty and allowances.[^siatka] Resident doctors’ pay is set by a regulation of the minister of health — we list it [by specialty](/en/data/residents-pay-by-specialty/). Everything above the minimum is agreed between the hospital and the doctor.

No register collects contract rates. Most of what is known comes from a one-off AOTMiT compilation from February 2026, known from press reports: the median monthly payment to a specialist under a single contract was <Money value={25595} /> gross.[^aotmit] That is the amount from one facility. The agency has no data linking payments to the same person from different hospitals,[^wywiad] so a doctor working in several places appears in the compilation as several separate entries.

Since 5 August 2026 the minister of health may request from facilities pay data linked to the PESEL number and the licence to practise (PWZ) number — in other words, data that can be summed for one person. The act does not, however, provide for their publication.[^ustawa-972] We describe the details in our analysis [The pay data act](/en/analysis/pay-data-act-vs-public-register/).

## Czechia: a government table in public facilities

Czechia has compulsory health insurance run by seven funds; the largest covers more than half of the population.[^soh-cz]

A doctor’s pay depends on who runs the facility. In public facilities — state, regional and municipal bodies that are not companies — a doctor receives a *plat*. Its base part, the tarif, is set by Government Regulation No. 341/2017 Sb., in which doctors, dentists and pharmacists have a separate table. The rate depends on the class, i.e. the requirements of the post, and on the step, i.e. years of practice.[^cz-tabela] A doctor with a specialisation is usually placed in class 14.[^medijob] Companies and private facilities pay contractual wages (*mzda*) and are not bound by the table; the medical statistics office ÚZIS counts the two groups separately.[^medijob][^uzis]

What is public, then, is the table of rates and averages from official statistics. We give the amounts from the table below, in the comparison of [public pay tables](#public-pay-tables-in-figures). ÚZIS reports that in 2024 a doctor (together with dentists) earned on average <Money value={126109} currency="CZK" /> a month in public facilities and <Money value={120123} currency="CZK" /> in companies and private facilities.[^uzis][^nbp] The average is clearly higher than the tarif even at the top step of the table. The tarif is therefore only part of the pay, and these data do not show how much of the average comes from allowances, bonuses and overtime. Nor is it known how much a specific doctor earns.

A 2023 dispute showed how much work beyond normal hours matters. An amendment to the Labour Code doubled the permitted limit of voluntary overtime for doctors to 832 hours a year. Doctors protested, and the agreement between the government and their organisations of 8 December 2023 brought raises of CZK 5,000–15,000, depending on qualifications.[^zd-2023]

A pay table in public facilities existed before: the 2017 regulation replaced Regulation No. 564/2006 Sb. and its amendments.[^cz-tabela] In February 2011, after the “Děkujeme, odcházíme” (“Thank you, we’re leaving”) campaign, in which more than 3,800 of about 20,000 doctors handed in their notice, the minister of health agreed a memorandum with their organisations: doctors’ pay was to rise by CZK 5,000–8,000 a month, with the revised tarifs applying from 1 March 2011. The memorandum also assumed that from 2013 the average doctor’s pay would reach between 1.5 and 3 times the national average wage.[^ct24] The protest was thus about the level of pay, not about the transparency of the table.

## Germany: a full table in hospitals, practices outside it

In Germany 89.0% of the population has statutory health insurance with one of 96 sickness funds, and 10.4% has substitutive private insurance (2024 data). Outpatient care is provided mainly by doctors’ practices, inpatient care by hospitals.[^soh-de]

Doctors employed in hospitals are paid under collective agreements. One example is the agreement for municipal hospitals (TV-Ärzte/VKA), which the doctors’ union Marburger Bund publishes in full. It has four groups — doctor without a specialisation, specialist, senior physician (Oberarzt) responsible for part of a department, and deputy head of department — each with steps based on years of work. Standard working time is 40 hours a week.[^tv-aerzte]

Heads of department (Chefarzt) stay outside the table when their terms are set by an individual contract — the agreement then does not cover them. With doctors in the two highest groups, after long enough at the top step, a hospital may also agree pay above the table.[^tv-aerzte] The agreement does not give the amounts of such contracts.

A doctor with their own practice does not receive a salary: their income is the practice’s result after costs. Such incomes are known only as averages for groups of doctors, like the OECD data described above.[^oecd-pensje]

## Austria: tables of the federal states, contracted and private practices

Austrian health insurance covers 99.9% of the population. Since a 2020 reform it has been run by three main institutions, including the employees’ fund ÖGK. Hospitals are planned by the federal states, which run about 75% of acute hospital capacity, while outpatient care is provided mainly by doctors in their own practices.[^soh-at]

The pay of hospital doctors is set by each federal state separately, so there are several tables. One example is the table of the Styrian hospital company KAGes, in which specialists and senior physicians (Oberarzt) are in one group (SI/N4) with 19 steps.[^kages] Two extra payments a year come on top of the monthly salary: under Styria’s civil service law an employee is entitled every quarter to an extra payment of half a monthly salary.[^ldbr]

Outside hospitals a doctor works either as a contracted doctor (Kassenarzt), who has a contract with a fund and whose patients pay nothing, or as an elective doctor (Wahlarzt), without such a contract. A Wahlarzt’s patients pay themselves, and ÖGK usually reimburses 80% of the rate it would have paid a contracted doctor — often much less than the bill. The number of contracted doctors has stagnated, while the number of elective doctors has grown by 61% since 2004.[^soh-at]

Nobody publishes the incomes of individual practices. The IHS institute calculated them in a report published in 2025 by the association of Austrian social insurance institutions, using pseudonymised data from the ministry of finance and the social insurance system. The median annual income of a doctor with contracts with all funds was €201,306 before tax in 2022, and of an elective doctor €100,849. Both figures also include employment income. The authors note that there is generally little transparency about incomes in Austria and that, unlike in Germany, the costs of running a practice are not studied.[^ihs]

## Sweden: no table, but pay on request

The Swedish system is financed mainly from taxes. Twenty-one regions are responsible for financing and for most services, and about 44% of primary care centres are run by private providers.[^soh-se]

Doctors employed by the regions are covered by a collective agreement, but there is no table of rates. The agreement and the region’s pay policy require pay to be set individually and to differ between employees.[^se-uklad] Official statistics give averages: according to the statistics office SCB, a specialist earned on average <Money value={98000} currency="SEK" /> a month in 2025, on a full-time basis.[^scb][^nbp]

The pay of a specific person employed by a public body, on the other hand, is an official document. The principle of public access (*offentlighetsprincipen*) derives from the Freedom of the Press Act: everyone has the right to see official documents, and the authority may not ask who is requesting them or why.[^tf] The trade union ST explains that pay data in public authorities are public and anyone may request them, whereas there is no such right at private employers.[^st] Exceptions are set out in the Public Access to Information and Secrecy Act.[^regeringen] The pay of doctors in private clinics, including those funded by the regions, therefore remains outside this principle.

## Public pay tables in figures

The tables from Germany, Czechia and Austria give the base pay of a salaried specialist in a public hospital.[^tv-aerzte][^cz-tabela][^kages] Each covers a different group of hospitals and a different scope of duties, and none includes on-call duty or allowances. That is why we do not calculate differences between countries from them and do not set the Polish minimum, which is a floor rather than a rate, beside them. Sweden has no table; the Polish minimum and the Swedish average are given in the country sections.

## How many doctors per population

According to the OECD, in 2023 Poland had 3.9 practising doctors per 1,000 population — equal to the OECD average and below the European Union average of 4.3.[^oecd-lekarze][^soh-pl] That is the fewest of the five countries compared: Austria had 5.5 doctors, Germany 4.7, Sweden 4.5 (2022 data) and Czechia 4.2.[^oecd-lekarze]

The OECD counts doctors who treat patients, in most countries including doctors in specialist training.[^oecd-lekarze] Polish sources count doctors in several ways and each gives a different number — we explain the differences in our analysis [Why Poland is short of doctors](/en/analysis/why-poland-is-short-of-doctors/).

The number of doctors alone does not determine access to care. In Austria, which has the most doctors of these five countries, a report by the European Commission and the OECD points to an uneven distribution of doctors, 80 unfilled positions for family doctors with a fund contract in mid-2024, and doctors moving to practices without such a contract.[^soh-at]

## What this means for Poland

Relative to the average wage, a salaried Polish specialist earns more than in Czechia and Sweden and less than in Germany. That figure, however, does not cover contracts, on which most Polish specialists work, so it does not say how much the average specialist earns.

In Czechia, Germany and Austria the reference point for pay in a public hospital is a public table, and in Sweden it is the pay of a specific person, available on request. In Poland only the statutory minimum for employees plays that role.

None of these countries publishes the total income of one person from different sources. Austria comes closest, but only in a study: the IHS institute added up doctors’ income from practice and employment on pseudonymised data, without revealing whom it concerns.[^ihs] We call for such a total to be published in Poland: [the total paid to each person from public money, under a permanent identifier, without names](/en/demand/).

## Method

We compare pay with a single measure from the OECD report *Health at a Glance 2025*: the ratio of a specialist’s average annual gross pay to the average wage of a full-time employee in the country, for 2023 or the nearest year with data. We take the number of practising doctors from the same report, and the EU average from Poland’s profile in *State of Health in the EU 2025*, as in our analysis of the doctor shortage. The descriptions of the systems are based on the country profiles from the same series, and the rates on the texts of regulations and collective agreements and on employers’ tables.

Amounts in zloty and in Czech and Swedish koruna are given in the original currency, with an approximate euro equivalent at the National Bank of Poland mid rate shown at the bottom of the page.[^nbp] We write about mechanisms, not individuals, so the text names institutions rather than people.
